What can a graph's eigenvalues reveal about its combinatorial shape?

A matrix turns local adjacency into a linear operator. Its eigenvalues can then certify connectedness, count closed walks, bound independent sets, and control cuts. The challenge is choosing the correct matrix and hypotheses: an adjacency eigenvalue, a Laplacian eigenvalue, and a normalized Laplacian eigenvalue have different meanings.

For the chapter foundation, all graphs are finite, simple, undirected, and unweighted. The adjacency matrix $A$ has entry one exactly on adjacent vertex pairs. The degree matrix $D$ is diagonal, and $L=D-A$ is the Laplacian. Its kernel consists of vectors constant on each connected component. For a $d$-regular graph, order adjacency eigenvalues as $d=\lambda_1\geq\lambda_2\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_n$. The Laplacian eigenvalues are $d-\lambda_i$, and the normalized Laplacian eigenvalues are $\mu_i=1-\lambda_i/d$, in increasing order when $d\gt0$.

Two established tools will support the final investigation. For a regular graph with an edge and least adjacency eigenvalue $\tau\lt0$, Hoffman's bound is $\alpha(G)\leq n(-\tau)/(d-\tau)$, where $\alpha$ is the maximum independent-set size. For a connected regular graph, conductance $\Phi$ satisfies $\mu_2/2\leq\Phi\leq\sqrt{2\mu_2}$, using the definition in 31.5. The optional sections give proofs and further applications; their advanced arguments are not required to complete the foundation investigation.

Motivating Problem

What can a graph's eigenvalues reveal about its combinatorial shape?

Use the Petersen graph in its two-subset model. Its vertices are $12,13,14,15,23,24,25,34,35,45$, where $ab$ denotes $\{a,b\}$. Join two vertices exactly when their two-subsets are disjoint. Every vertex has three neighbors; for example $12$ is adjacent to $34,35,45$.

The graph has ten vertices and fifteen edges. Adjacent vertices have no common neighbor, while nonadjacent distinct vertices have exactly one. Consequently

$$ A^2=2I-A+J, $$

where $J$ is the all-ones matrix. On the constant vector, $A$ has eigenvalue three. On its orthogonal complement, $J$ vanishes, so every eigenvalue solves $x^2+x-2=0$, giving $1$ or $-2$. The multiplicities, found from dimension and trace, are five and four. Thus the adjacency spectrum is $3,1^{[5]},(-2)^{[4]}$, where brackets denote multiplicity.

Hoffman's bound now gives $\alpha\leq10\cdot2/(3+2)=4$. The vertices $12,13,14,15$ have pairwise intersecting labels, hence are pairwise nonadjacent: they attain four. A matrix certificate and a concrete set together prove $\alpha=4$. Revisit why the least eigenvalue constrains an independent set, and why a numerical spectrum alone needs an exact justification before it certifies an integer bound.

31.1 Adjacency, Laplacian, and incidence matrices

Optional extension; not required to complete the chapter foundation. Adjacency measures propagation; the Laplacian measures variation across edges. An incidence factorization explains why the latter is positive semidefinite.

Give each edge an arbitrary orientation. The signed vertex-edge incidence matrix $B$ has a column with $-1$ at its tail, $+1$ at its head, and zeros elsewhere. Each edge contributes one to the two diagonal entries and minus one to the corresponding off-diagonal entries, proving

$$ L=BB^{\mathsf T},\qquad x^{\mathsf T}Lx=\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}(x_u-x_v)^2\geq0. $$

Reversing an edge negates its column and leaves $BB^{\mathsf T}$ unchanged. This identity proves that $Lx=0$ exactly when $x$ is constant on each connected component, so the nullity counts components. The unsigned incidence matrix instead satisfies $BB^{\mathsf T}=D+A$; orientation and sign conventions cannot be omitted.

When all degrees are positive, define the symmetric normalized Laplacian and random-walk matrix by

$$ \mathcal L=I-D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2},\qquad P=D^{-1}A. $$

They are related by similarity between $P$ and $D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}$. Isolated vertices require a separately specified convention; this chapter's normalized statements exclude them. For nonregular graphs, $L$ is not a scalar multiple of $\mathcal L$, and adjacency eigenvalues cannot simply be subtracted from one fixed degree.

Solved example. For the path $1-2-3$, orient edges $1\to2$ and $2\to3$:

$$ A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad L=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&1\end{pmatrix}. $$

The adjacency spectrum is $\sqrt2,0,-\sqrt2$. The Laplacian eigenvectors $(1,1,1)$, $(1,0,-1)$, and $(1,-2,1)$ have eigenvalues $0,1,3$. The normalized Laplacian has spectrum $0,1,2$. These differences illustrate why normalization matters.

The entry $(A^k)_{uv}$ counts walks of length $k$ from $u$ to $v$: induction appends the last edge. In particular $\operatorname{tr}(A^2)=2\lvert E\rvert$ and $\operatorname{tr}(A^3)=6$ times the number of triangles. These are walk counts, not generally counts of simple cycles for larger $k$.

Use signed incidence for $L=BB^{\mathsf T}$, and distinguish $L$ from $\mathcal L$. The adjacency-to-Laplacian shift $d-\lambda_i$ is valid for regular graphs; normalized formulas here require positive degrees.

Exercise. Reverse the first path edge and recompute $BB^{\mathsf T}$. Also determine the Laplacian nullity after adjoining one isolated vertex. Hint: A column sign cancels in the outer product. Solution: The first column becomes $(1,-1,0)^{\mathsf T}$, and the product is the same $L$. The extra isolated vertex contributes a zero block, so nullity becomes two, matching the two connected components.

31.2 Eigenvalue interlacing

Optional extension; not required to complete the chapter foundation. Restricting a symmetric matrix to a coordinate subspace cannot create completely unconstrained eigenvalues. The variational principle makes this precise.

Let a real symmetric $n\times n$ matrix $M$ have decreasing eigenvalues $\lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_n$. If $N$ is an $m\times m$ principal submatrix with decreasing eigenvalues $\theta_1\geq\cdots\geq\theta_m$, then

$$ \lambda_i\geq\theta_i\geq\lambda_{i+n-m},\qquad1\leq i\leq m. $$

More generally this holds for the compression $Q^{\mathsf T}MQ$ when $Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I_m$. The Courant–Fischer principle characterizes eigenvalues by maximizing and minimizing the Rayleigh quotient $x^{\mathsf T}Mx/x^{\mathsf T}x$ over subspaces. Restricting those subspaces to $\operatorname{im}Q$ gives the first inequality; its codimension $n-m$ gives the shifted second inequality. This is the proof mechanism, with the standard min–max theorem as a linear-algebra prerequisite.

For an induced subgraph, adjacency is a principal submatrix. Its Laplacian usually is not the corresponding principal submatrix of the original Laplacian: deleted edges change the retained degrees. Confusing these two operations gives incorrect interlacing arguments.

Proof Workshop

Eigenvalue interlacing: a worked example

Delete one vertex from the four-cycle $C_4$. The resulting induced graph is the path $P_3$. The adjacency spectra are $2,0,0,-2$ and $\sqrt2,0,-\sqrt2$ respectively. Here $n=4,m=3$, so every required inequality is

$$ 2\geq\sqrt2\geq0,\qquad 0\geq0\geq0,\qquad 0\geq-\sqrt2\geq-2. $$

The matrices are real symmetric, and the path adjacency is literally a principal submatrix, so all hypotheses hold. The middle equality checks the repeated eigenvalue correctly; one must retain multiplicities when ordering a spectrum.

For a second compression, partition Petersen vertices into $S=\{12,13,14,15\}$ and its six-vertex complement. Every vertex in $S$ has three neighbors outside; every outside vertex has two in $S$ and one outside. The quotient is $\begin{pmatrix}0&3\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$. It is not symmetric, but using normalized cell indicators gives the symmetric compression

$$ Q^{\mathsf T}AQ=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt6\\\sqrt6&1\end{pmatrix}, $$

with eigenvalues three and minus two. Because the neighbor counts are constant on each cell, this partition is equitable; cell-constant vectors form an invariant subspace, so these eigenvalues lift to actual adjacency eigenvalues. Merely averaging neighbor counts for a nonequitable partition would give interlacing, without that lifting conclusion.

Exercise. Delete an endpoint from $P_3$ and check interlacing for the resulting single edge. Hint: Its eigenvalues are $1,-1$. Solution: $\sqrt2\geq1\geq0$ and $0\geq-1\geq-\sqrt2$. Its Laplacian is $\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$, whereas the retained principal block of the original path Laplacian is $\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$ after deleting vertex one. These are different matrices.

31.3 Spectral graph bounds

Optional extension; not required to complete the chapter foundation. A spectral inequality becomes useful when paired with a combinatorial witness and a clear direction of comparison.

For any graph with $n$ vertices, $m$ edges, and maximum degree $\Delta$, the largest adjacency eigenvalue satisfies

$$ \frac{2m}{n}\leq\lambda_1\leq\Delta. $$

The lower bound uses the all-ones vector in the Rayleigh quotient. For the upper bound, take an eigenvector and a coordinate of largest absolute value: $\lvert\lambda\rvert\lvert x_u\rvert\leq d(u)\max_v\lvert x_v\rvert\leq\Delta\lvert x_u\rvert$. This bounds every eigenvalue's absolute value. In a regular graph both sides agree at $d$. If a graph contains a clique of size $r$, its induced adjacency has largest eigenvalue $r-1$, so interlacing gives $\omega(G)\leq\lambda_1+1$.

Solved example. Petersen is cubic, so $\lambda_1=3$ and the clique bound gives $\omega\leq4$, a weak estimate. Its spectral triangle count is

$$ 6t=\operatorname{tr}(A^3)=3^3+5\cdot1^3+4\cdot(-2)^3=0. $$

Thus there are no triangles, improving the clique bound to $\omega\leq2$. Any edge witnesses $\omega=2$. Its edge count also checks spectrally: $(3^2+5\cdot1^2+4\cdot(-2)^2)/2=15$.

Hoffman's independent-set bound, stated in the foundation and proved in 31.9, gives another exact conclusion: $\alpha\leq4$, attained by all two-subsets containing one fixed label. The figure highlights this witness. Every drawn edge joins disjoint two-subsets; geometric line crossings are not vertices. This makes the spectral certificate and the finite object directly comparable.

Spectra also have limits. The connected star $K_{1,4}$ and the disconnected graph $C_4\sqcup K_1$ share adjacency spectrum $2,0,0,0,-2$. Their independent-set sizes are four and three. Thus adjacency spectrum alone does not determine connectedness or independence in general. Laplacian nullity distinguishes their component counts. For a regular graph, however, the multiplicity of adjacency eigenvalue $d$ does count components, because $L=dI-A$.

Exercise. Use the complement to obtain another clique bound for Petersen. Hint: The complement is six-regular, and on vectors perpendicular to $\mathbf1$ its eigenvalues are $-1-\lambda_i$. Solution: Its spectrum is $6,(-2)^{[5]},1^{[4]}$. Hoffman's bound gives $\alpha(\overline G)\leq10\cdot2/(6+2)=5/2$. Since the left side is an integer, $\omega(G)\leq2$, attained by an edge. Rounding and the direction of the inequality are essential.

31.4 Expander graphs

Optional extension; not required to complete the chapter foundation. Expansion means that small vertex sets have many connections leaving them. It measures resistance to bottlenecks, rather than merely the presence of a path between every pair.

For a $d$-regular graph, let $\partial S$ consist of edges with exactly one endpoint in $S$ and define

$$ h(G)=\min_{0\lt\lvert S\rvert\leq n/2}\frac{\lvert\partial S\rvert}{\lvert S\rvert}. $$

A bounded-degree expander family has increasing numbers of vertices and a uniform positive lower bound on $h$. A single small graph can illustrate expansion, but is not itself an infinite family. Vertex expansion is a different measure; bounded degree lets one compare the two because each outside vertex is incident to at most $d$ boundary edges.

For connected regular graphs, the one-sided normalized gap is $\mu_2=(d-\lambda_2)/d$. The absolute nonprincipal adjacency parameter is

$$ \Lambda=\max_{2\leq i\leq n}\lvert\lambda_i\rvert. $$

The first controls cuts through Cheeger inequalities; the second controls signed fluctuations and nonlazy random walks. A connected bipartite graph has eigenvalue $-d$, so $\Lambda=d$ even when its one-sided gap is large. These quantities must not both be called an unspecified “second eigenvalue.”

Solved example. For Petersen, $\mu_2=2/3$, $\Lambda=2$, and $h=1$, as established in 31.5. To remove periodicity issues, use the lazy random walk

$$ P_{\mathrm{lazy}}=\frac12(I+A/d). $$

Its nonconstant eigenvalues are $1-\mu_i/2$, all in $[0,1)$ for a connected graph. The uniform distribution $\pi$ is stationary. Spectral contraction on the zero-sum subspace and Cauchy–Schwarz give, from any starting vertex,

$$ \lVert p_t-\pi\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}} \leq\frac{\sqrt{n-1}}2(1-\mu_2/2)^t. $$

Indeed the initial Euclidean distance from uniform is $\sqrt{1-1/n}$, it contracts by the displayed eigenvalue factor, and total variation is half the $\ell^1$ distance, at most $\sqrt n/2$ times the Euclidean distance. For Petersen the eigenvalues of this walk are $1,(2/3)^{[5]},(1/6)^{[4]}$. At ten steps the bound is $(3/2)(2/3)^{10}\lt0.027$.

In contrast, the cycle $C_N$ has a consecutive set of $\lfloor N/2\rfloor$ vertices with only two boundary edges. Thus $h(C_N)\leq2/\lfloor N/2\rfloor\to0$: cycles do not form an expander family. Their normalized gap $1-\cos(2\pi/N)$ likewise tends to zero.

Exercise. Explain why the ordinary walk on $C_4$ fails to converge from a vertex although $\mu_2=1$. Hint: Its adjacency spectrum contains $-2$. Solution: The walk alternates the two bipartition classes, so a parity component persists. The lazy walk has spectrum $1,1/2,1/2,0$ and converges. The one-sided gap alone did not remove the nonlazy eigenvalue minus one.

31.5 Cheeger inequalities

Optional extension; not required to complete the chapter foundation. For a connected graph with positive degrees, let $\operatorname{vol}(S)=\sum_{v\in S}d(v)$ and define conductance by

$$ \Phi(G)=\min_{\varnothing\neq S\subsetneq V} \frac{\lvert\partial S\rvert}{\min(\operatorname{vol}(S),\operatorname{vol}(V\setminus S))}. $$

If $\mu_2$ is the second-smallest eigenvalue of $\mathcal L$, the standard Cheeger inequalities are $\mu_2/2\leq\Phi\leq\sqrt{2\mu_2}$. In a regular graph $\Phi=h/d$. The following proof treats that case in full; the general case uses degree-weighted means and norms. See Chung's treatment of isoperimetric problems for that weighted formulation.

Lower bound. For a set of size $s\leq n/2$, use $x=\mathbf1_S-(s/n)\mathbf1$, perpendicular to constants. Its energy is $\lvert\partial S\rvert$ and its squared norm is $s(1-s/n)$. The variational characterization gives

$$ \mu_2\leq\frac{\lvert\partial S\rvert}{d\,s(1-s/n)} \leq\frac{2\lvert\partial S\rvert}{ds}. $$

Minimizing over sets proves $\mu_2/2\leq\Phi$.

Upper bound and sweep mechanism. Take a nonzero real eigenvector $f$ satisfying $Lf=d\mu_2 f$ and $\sum_v f_v=0$. Choose a median $m$, so both strict sides contain at most $n/2$ vertices. If $m\leq0$, put $g=(f-m)_+$; if $m\geq0$, put $g=(m-f)_+$. Choose either branch when $m=0$. The chosen $g$ is nonzero and supported on at most half the vertices.

For the first branch, monotonicity of truncation gives

$$ \sum_{uv\in E}(g_u-g_v)^2 \leq\sum_{uv\in E}(g_u-g_v)(f_u-f_v) =g^{\mathsf T}Lf =d\mu_2\left(\sum_vg_v^2+m\sum_vg_v\right) \leq d\mu_2\sum_vg_v^2. $$

The second branch uses $-f$ and $-m$, giving the same result. This explicitly justifies the truncation step.

For thresholds $t\geq0$, let $S_t=\{v:g_v^2\gt t\}$. Integrating the boundary and size of these level sets gives

$$ \int_0^\infty\lvert\partial S_t\rvert\,dt =\sum_{uv\in E}\lvert g_u^2-g_v^2\rvert,\qquad \int_0^\infty d\lvert S_t\rvert\,dt=d\sum_vg_v^2. $$

Thus at least one nonempty level set has conductance at most the ratio of the two right sides. Cauchy–Schwarz and $(g_u+g_v)^2\leq2(g_u^2+g_v^2)$ give

$$ \sum_{uv\in E}\lvert g_u^2-g_v^2\rvert \leq\sqrt{\sum_{uv\in E}(g_u-g_v)^2} \sqrt{2d\sum_vg_v^2}. $$

The ratio is at most $\sqrt{2\mu_2}$, proving the upper inequality and explaining the spectral sweep algorithm.

Solved example. Petersen contains the five-cycle $12,34,15,23,45,12$. Its five vertices each have two neighbors inside and one outside, so its boundary has five edges and its conductance is $5/(3\cdot5)=1/3$. The lower inequality gives $\Phi\geq(2/3)/2=1/3$. Therefore $\Phi=1/3$ and $h=1$, proved without exhaustively searching all cuts. The upper estimate $\sqrt{4/3}$ is loose here and even exceeds one; a valid upper bound need not be informative.

Exercise. Compute the conductance of $C_4$ and compare both bounds. Hint: An adjacent pair has two leaving edges and volume four. Solution: That cut gives $1/2$; singletons give one, so $\Phi=1/2$. Since $\mu_2=1$, the lower bound is sharp and the upper bound $\sqrt2$ is loose. Using the unnormalized Laplacian eigenvalue two in the normalized formula would mix conventions.

31.6 Strongly regular graphs

Optional extension; not required to complete the chapter foundation. Strong regularity replaces arbitrary local common-neighbor counts by two constants. This forces a short matrix identity and usually only three distinct adjacency eigenvalues.

A strongly regular graph with parameters $(v,k,a,c)$ is a nonempty, noncomplete $k$-regular graph on $v$ vertices in which adjacent vertices have $a$ common neighbors and nonadjacent distinct vertices have $c$. The letters $a,c$ avoid confusion with spectral eigenvalues. Counting length-two walks gives

$$ A^2=kI+aA+c(J-I-A)=(k-c)I+(a-c)A+cJ. $$

Double-counting edges from the neighbors of a fixed vertex to its nonneighbors gives the necessary parameter relation

$$ k(k-a-1)=(v-k-1)c. $$

A neighbor has $k-a-1$ neighbors outside that fixed vertex's closed neighborhood; each nonneighbor has $c$ neighbors inside its neighborhood. These counts describe the same edges.

On $\mathbf1^\perp$, the nonprincipal eigenvalues solve

$$ x^2-(a-c)x-(k-c)=0. $$

When the graph is connected, the eigenvalue $k$ is simple, and multiplicities of the two roots are determined by their sum $v-1$ and the trace-zero equation. Parameter feasibility, including integral nonnegative multiplicities, is necessary but does not by itself guarantee existence of a graph.

Solved example. In the Petersen two-subset model, disjoint pairs use four labels, leaving only one: no two-subset can be disjoint from both, so $a=0$. Intersecting distinct pairs use three labels; the remaining two labels give the unique common neighbor, so $c=1$. Its parameters are $(10,3,0,1)$, and $3(3-0-1)=6=(10-3-1)\cdot1$. The equation is $x^2+x-2=0$. If multiplicities of $1,-2$ are $f,g$, then $f+g=9$ and $3+f-2g=0$, giving $f=5,g=4$.

Connectedness follows directly: disjoint pairs are adjacent, and intersecting distinct pairs have a common neighbor. Every pair is therefore at distance at most two. This proves the simple principal eigenvalue hypothesis before using it in the multiplicity calculation.

Exercise. Verify that $C_5$ is strongly regular and compute its nonprincipal eigenvalues. Hint: Adjacent vertices share none; every nonadjacent pair shares one neighbor. Solution: Its parameters are $(5,2,0,1)$, and the roots are $(-1\pm\sqrt5)/2$, each with multiplicity two. Together with eigenvalue two they give trace zero and squared-eigenvalue sum ten, matching its five edges.

31.7 Association schemes and Bose–Mesner algebras

Optional extension; not required to complete the chapter foundation. A graph records one relation between vertices. An association scheme records several relations whose adjacency matrices multiply within a common finite-dimensional algebra.

A symmetric association scheme on a finite set $X$ consists of zero-one symmetric matrices $A_0,\ldots,A_r$ satisfying $A_0=I$, $\sum_iA_i=J$, and

$$ A_iA_j=\sum_{k=0}^r p_{ij}^kA_k. $$

The matrices have disjoint supports; $p_{ij}^k$ counts intermediate vertices $z$ with $(x,z)$ in relation $i$ and $(z,y)$ in relation $j$ whenever $(x,y)$ is in relation $k$. Independence of this count from the chosen pair is the key hypothesis. The span is the Bose–Mesner algebra. Products are symmetric by the closure formula, so $A_iA_j=A_jA_i$; commuting real symmetric matrices admit simultaneous orthogonal diagonalization.

Solved example. Petersen gives a two-class scheme with $A_0=I$, $A_1=A$, $A_2=J-I-A$. Direct substitution yields

$$ A_1^2=3A_0+A_2,\qquad A_1A_2=2A_1+2A_2,\qquad A_2^2=6A_0+4A_1+3A_2. $$

Their simultaneous eigenvalues are

Common eigenspaceDimension$A_0$ eigenvalue$A_1$ eigenvalue$A_2$ eigenvalue

Eigenvalue-one space of $A$$5$$1$$1$$-2$
Eigenvalue-minus-two space of $A$$4$$1$$-2$$1$

For nonconstant vectors, $J$ vanishes, so the last column is $-1$ minus the $A_1$ eigenvalue. The primitive orthogonal projectors are

$$ E_0=J/10,\qquad E_1=(A+2I-5E_0)/3,\qquad E_2=(I-A+2E_0)/3. $$

They satisfy $E_iE_j=\delta_{ij}E_i$, $\sum_iE_i=I$, and have traces $1,5,4$. This gives a second basis of the same algebra: relation matrices describe combinatorial incidences, while projectors describe spectral components. Ordinary matrix multiplication and entrywise multiplication are different operations; do not read the displayed product constants as entrywise identities. For broader background, see Brouwer–Haemers' author-hosted text.

Exercise. Check $A_1A_2=2A_1+2A_2$ on each common eigenspace. Hint: Multiply the two eigenvalues in each row. Solution: The left values are $18,-2,-2$, and the right values are $2(3+6)=18$, $2(1-2)=-2$, and $2(-2+1)=-2$. Since the common eigenspaces span all vectors, these checks prove the matrix identity once simultaneous diagonalization and the table are established.

31.8 Distance-regular graphs

Optional extension; not required to complete the chapter foundation. Distance regularity means that the numbers of neighbors moving closer to, staying equally far from, or moving farther from a base vertex depend only on the current distance.

Let a connected graph have diameter $D$. For vertices $x,y$ at distance $i$, let $c_i,a_i,b_i$ count neighbors of $y$ at distances $i-1,i,i+1$ from $x$. A graph is distance-regular if these numbers depend only on $i$, not on $x,y$. It is then regular of degree $k$, with $c_i+a_i+b_i=k$, $c_0=0$, and $b_D=0$. The intersection array is $\{b_0,\ldots,b_{D-1};c_1,\ldots,c_D\}$.

Let $k_i$ be the number of vertices at distance $i$ from a fixed vertex. Counting edges between consecutive layers gives $k_i b_i=k_{i+1}c_{i+1}$. The distance matrices $A_i$, with entries one on pairs at distance $i$, satisfy

$$ AA_i=b_{i-1}A_{i-1}+a_iA_i+c_{i+1}A_{i+1}, $$

omitting nonexistent terms. Since $c_{i+1}\gt0$ when $i\lt D$, this recurrence expresses each $A_i$ as a polynomial in $A$. The distance relations form a symmetric association scheme. The standard theorem that a connected distance-regular graph has exactly $D+1$ distinct eigenvalues follows from this recurrence and the independent distance matrices; its full algebraic argument is omitted here.

Solved example. Petersen has distance layers of sizes $1,3,6$. Its intersection array is $\{3,2;1,1\}$: a vertex in layer one has one neighbor back, none in its layer, and two forward; a vertex in layer two has one back and two in its own layer. The layer quotient is

$$ Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&3&0\\1&0&2\\0&1&2\end{pmatrix}. $$

Its characteristic polynomial is $(x-3)(x-1)(x+2)$. The quotient yields the distinct eigenvalues, not their multiplicities in the ten-vertex graph. Because $k_i b_i=k_{i+1}c_{i+1}$, conjugation by $\operatorname{diag}(1,\sqrt3,\sqrt6)$ makes the quotient symmetric, with off-diagonal entries $\sqrt3$ and $\sqrt2$. The recurrence gives $A^2=3I+A_2$ and $AA_2=2A+2A_2$, exactly the scheme identities from 31.7.

Exercise. Find the intersection array and spectrum of the cube graph $Q_3$. Hint: Label vertices by binary strings of length three and join strings differing in one coordinate. Solution: At Hamming distance $i$, there are $i$ ways to move back and $3-i$ to move forward, with none in the same layer. The array is $\{3,2,1;1,2,3\}$ and layer sizes are $1,3,3,1$. Functions $(-1)^{a\cdot x}$ are adjacency eigenvectors of eigenvalue $3-2\lvert a\rvert$, yielding $3,1^{[3]},(-1)^{[3]},-3$. This also verifies bipartiteness spectrally.

31.9 Spectral methods for coloring and independence

Optional extension; not required to complete the chapter foundation. An independent set has zero internal adjacency energy. Centering its indicator makes that fact comparable with a least eigenvalue.

Let $G$ be $d$-regular on $n$ vertices with at least one edge, and let $\tau=\lambda_n\lt0$. For an independent set $S$ of size $s\gt0$, put $z=\mathbf1_S-(s/n)\mathbf1$. Then

$$ \lVert z\rVert^2=s-s^2/n,\qquad z^{\mathsf T}Az=-ds^2/n. $$

The second identity follows by expanding, using $A\mathbf1=d\mathbf1$ and $\mathbf1_S^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf1_S=0$. The least-eigenvalue inequality gives

$$ -ds^2/n\geq\tau(s-s^2/n), \qquad \alpha(G)\leq\frac{n(-\tau)}{d-\tau}. $$

Equality forces the centered indicator into the $\tau$ eigenspace. Then a vertex outside $S$ has $-\tau$ neighbors in $S$, while a vertex inside has zero. This follows by substituting the two possible values of $z$ into $Az=\tau z$; it is a useful structural check on an extremal set.

A proper coloring partitions the vertices into independent sets, so $n\leq\chi(G)\alpha(G)$. Consequently the regular-graph Hoffman coloring bound is

$$ \chi(G)\geq1-d/\tau. $$

Round an independence upper bound down and a coloring lower bound up when drawing integer conclusions. These formulas require the stated regularity and negative denominator; the empty graph is handled separately.

Solved example. Petersen has $d=3,\tau=-2$, so $\alpha\leq4$ and $\chi\geq5/2$, hence $\chi\geq3$. The following independent color classes give a proper three-coloring:

$$ \{12,13,14,15\},\qquad \{23,24,25\},\qquad \{34,35,45\}. $$

Within each class labels share a common element or belong to the three pairs on a three-element set, so no two are disjoint. Thus $\chi=3$. The first class attains the independence bound, and every outside vertex has two neighbors in it, as the equality condition predicts.

For $C_5$, the least eigenvalue is $-(1+\sqrt5)/2$. Both the independence upper bound and the unrounded coloring lower bound are $\sqrt5$: after rounding, $\alpha\leq2$ and $\chi\geq3$. Nonadjacent vertices give an independent pair, and an ordinary three-coloring gives equality in the integer conclusions. The real-valued inequalities themselves need not be equalities.

Exercise. Apply the bounds to $K_{3,3}$. Hint: Its spectrum is $3,0^{[4]},-3$. Solution: $\alpha\leq6\cdot3/(3+3)=3$ and $\chi\geq2$. Each side of the bipartition gives an independent triple, and the bipartition is a two-coloring. These sharp conclusions coexist with $\Lambda=d$: good independence bounds do not automatically mean two-sided pseudorandomness.

31.10 Spectral pseudorandomness

Optional extension; not required to complete the chapter foundation. A deterministic graph can distribute edges approximately like a uniform density model. The quantitative meaning here is discrepancy between vertex sets, not independent random choices of edges.

For a regular graph set $\Lambda=\max_{i\geq2}\lvert\lambda_i\rvert$, retaining all nonprincipal eigenvalues even if $d$ repeats. Define the ordered edge count

$$ e(S,T)=\mathbf1_S^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf1_T. $$

For disjoint sets this counts each crossing edge once. For $S=T$ it counts internal edges twice. For overlapping sets the matrix definition removes ambiguity. The expander mixing lemma states the refined bound

$$ \left\lvert e(S,T)-\frac{d\lvert S\rvert\lvert T\rvert}{n}\right\rvert \leq\Lambda\sqrt{\lvert S\rvert(1-\lvert S\rvert/n)\lvert T\rvert(1-\lvert T\rvert/n)} \leq\Lambda\sqrt{\lvert S\rvert\lvert T\rvert}. $$

Proof. Center the indicators as $x=\mathbf1_S-(\lvert S\rvert/n)\mathbf1$ and $y=\mathbf1_T-(\lvert T\rvert/n)\mathbf1$. The discrepancy equals $x^{\mathsf T}Ay$. On $\mathbf1^\perp$, the operator norm of $A$ is $\Lambda$, so its absolute value is at most $\Lambda\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert$. Computing the two squared norms gives the refined expression. This is a proof for all sets simultaneously, not a random-sampling argument.

The expected density term is $d/n$, arising from the rank-one matrix $(d/n)J$. It includes diagonal entries even though the actual simple graph has no loops; the discrepancy term accounts for that. It is therefore not exactly the marginal edge probability $d/(n-1)$ in a uniform random regular graph. Strong relative guarantees require the density term to dominate the error; small sets may receive a loose bound even when $\Lambda/d$ is small.

Solved example. In Petersen, take $S=\{12,13,14,15\}$ and $T=V\setminus S$. Then $\lvert S\rvert=4$, $\lvert T\rvert=6$, and $e(S,T)=12$. The density term is $3\cdot4\cdot6/10=36/5$, so the discrepancy is $24/5$. The refined upper bound is

$$ 2\sqrt{4(1-4/10)\cdot6(1-6/10)}=24/5. $$

It is exact. With $S=T$ equal to the independent four-set, the actual ordered count is zero, the density term is $24/5$, and the same refined error again gives equality. The centered indicator lies in the minus-two eigenspace, explaining the saturation. Petersen is a small illustration; its ratio $\Lambda/d=2/3$ is not an asymptotically vanishing pseudorandomness parameter.

The matrix viewpoint connects the chapter: $A=(d/n)J+R$ with $\lVert R\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\Lambda$; regularity also gives $L=dI-A=BB^{\mathsf T}$. Thus incidence energy controls cut variation, while the same adjacency eigenspaces control discrepancy. One-sided gap bounds and absolute discrepancy bounds use different portions of that spectrum.

Exercise. Find $\Lambda/d$ for $K_n$ and explain what its small value means. Hint: $A=J-I$. Solution: The nonprincipal eigenvalues are all minus one, so $\Lambda/d=1/(n-1)\to0$. This deterministic dense family has uniformly small spectral discrepancy relative to its degree. It demonstrates that pseudorandom edge distribution does not require the graph itself to have been sampled randomly.

A cross-disciplinary application is spectral partitioning of a network. The incidence energy $\lVert B^{\mathsf T}f\rVert^2$ measures disagreement across edges; a low normalized eigenvalue can identify a bottleneck, and sweeping its eigenvector coordinates produces candidate cuts with the guarantee proved in 31.5. Meanwhile a small absolute nonprincipal adjacency norm bounds deviations from uniform edge distribution. These certificates help distinguish a separable network from one with broadly distributed connections. For Petersen, the balanced five-cycle cut is already an exact eigenvector witness: its centered indicator has adjacency eigenvalue one and Laplacian eigenvalue two. A certified spectrum supplies the bound, and the explicit cut supplies its combinatorial interpretation.
