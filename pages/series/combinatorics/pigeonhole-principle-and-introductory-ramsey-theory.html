Why Must Six People Contain a Familiar or Unfamiliar Triple?

Suppose six people attend a gathering. For every pair of individuals, we record exactly one of two mutual relationships: either they know each other (friends), or they do not (strangers). Must there always exist a group of three people who are all mutual friends, or three people who are all mutual strangers?

This classic problem—often called the Theorem on Friends and Strangers—marks the primary entry point into Ramsey Theory, a branch of combinatorics concerned with unavoidable order in large discrete systems. To analyze this rigorously, we translate the social scenario directly into graph-theoretic terms:

Translating the Party Problem to Graph Theory
  • People $\to$ Vertices: The 6 attendees are modeled as 6 vertices of a complete graph $K_6$, where an edge joins every pair of vertices.
  • Relationships $\to$ Edge Colors: Every edge is colored either Red (mutual friends) or Blue (mutual strangers).
  • Target Structure $\to$ Monochromatic Triangles: A trio of mutual friends or mutual strangers corresponds to a monochromatic triangle ($K_3$).

The fundamental question can thus be rephrased: Is it impossible to color the 15 edges of $K_6$ with two colors(Red non-zero and Blue non-zero) without creating at least one single-color(Red or Blue) triangle? As we will prove using the Pigeonhole Principle, the answer is a definitive yes.

Motivating Problem Walkthrough

Proof Walkthrough: Why $R(3,3) = 6$ (The Party Problem)

Ramsey Theory guarantees that complete order emerges from sufficient disorder. The classic Party Problem asks: "What is the minimum number of people $N$ required at a party to guarantee that at least 3 people are mutual friends or 3 people are mutual strangers?" In graph theory, this is modeled as finding the Ramsey number $R(3,3)$, where we 2-color the edges of a complete graph $K_N$ using Red (representing mutual friends) and Blue (representing mutual strangers), searching for a monochromatic triangle ($K_3$).

Part 1: Upper Bound Proof ($R(3,3) \le 6$)

Take a complete graph on 6 vertices ($K_6$) with labeled vertices $A, B, C, D, E, F$. Every pair of vertices is connected by an edge colored either Red or Blue.

  1. Degree Analysis & Pigeonhole Principle:
    Focus on vertex $A$. Since $K_6$ has 6 vertices, vertex $A$ is connected to the remaining 5 vertices by 5 distinct edges: $(AB, AC, AD, AE, AF)$. By the Generalized Pigeonhole Principle, dividing $5$ edges into $2$ color categories (Red or Blue) guarantees that at least: $$\left\lceil \frac{5}{2} \right\rceil = 3\text{ edges}$$ must share the exact same color. Without loss of generality, assume these 3 majority-colored edges connected to $A$ are Red, and lead to neighbor vertices $B, C,$ and $D$.
  2. Examine the Sub-Graph $\{B, C, D\}$:
    Now consider the 3 interconnecting edges between these target vertices: $BC, BD,$ and $CD$. There are two exhaustive, mutually exclusive possibilities for how these 3 edges are colored:
    Case A: At Least One Red Edge

    Suppose at least one edge among $\{BC, BD, CD\}$ is colored Red. For example, let $BD$ be Red. Since $AB$ and $AD$ are already Red, the three vertices $\{A, B, D\}$ form a solid Red triangle ($\triangle ABD$) representing 3 mutual friends!

    Case B: Zero Red Edges

    Suppose none of the edges among $\{BC, BD, CD\}$ are Red. Since every edge must be 2-colored, all three edges $BC, BD,$ and $CD$ are forced to be Blue. Thus, the vertices $\{B, C, D\}$ form a solid Blue triangle ($\triangle BCD$) representing 3 mutual strangers!

Upper Bound Conclusion: In both cases, a monochromatic triangle ($K_3$) is guaranteed to exist. Therefore, 6 people are always sufficient: $R(3,3) \le 6$.
Part 2: Lower Bound Proof ($R(3,3) > 5$)

To prove that 6 is the sharp minimum threshold, we must construct a counterexample showing that a 5-person group ($K_5$) can avoid both Red and Blue triangles entirely ($R(3,3) > 5$).

The $C_5$ Construction (5-Cycle Graph):
Arrange 5 vertices in a circle labeled $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$. Color the edges as follows:
  • Color the 5 outer boundary edges $(12, 23, 34, 45, 51)$ Red (forming a 5-cycle graph $C_5$).
  • Color the 5 inner diagonal chords $(13, 24, 35, 42, 52)$ Blue (forming a complementary 5-cycle graph $\bar{C}_5$).
Resulting Structure:

No Red triangle exists because the smallest cycle in $C_5$ has length 5. Likewise, no Blue triangle exists because the complement of $C_5$ is also isomorphic to a 5-cycle ($\bar{C}_5 \cong C_5$), which also contains no 3-cycles!

Summary & Exact Value: Since $K_5$ can be 2-colored without any monochromatic triangles ($R(3,3) > 5$) and $K_6$ always forces at least one monochromatic triangle ($R(3,3) \le 6$), we establish the exact sharp threshold: $$\mathbf{R(3,3) = 6}$$

3.1 Basic Pigeonhole Principle

The Pigeonhole Principle (historically known as Dirichlet's Box Principle) states an intuitively obvious yet deeply powerful truth: If more objects than container boxes are distributed into those boxes, then at least one box must contain two or more objects.

The Basic Pigeonhole Principle (Formal)

If $n+1$ or more objects are placed into $n$ distinct boxes (where $n \ge 1$), then at least one box contains two or more objects. In set theory, a function $f: A \to B$ between finite sets cannot be injective (one-to-one) if $|A| > |B|$.

The "boxes" in combinatorial proofs are abstract categories rather than physical bins. Identifying the correct choice of boxes is the key creative step.

Classic Applications:

  • Birth Months: Among 13 people, at least two were born in the same month (13 objects, 12 month boxes).
  • Modular Remainders: Every integer leaves one of 5 possible remainders ($\{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$) when divided by 5. Therefore, among any 6 integers, at least two share the same remainder $r$. Their difference $(5u + r) - (5v + r) = 5(u - v)$ is guaranteed to be divisible by 5!

Worked Exercise: Pairs Summing to 21

Problem: Show that choosing 11 distinct integers from $\{1, 2, \dots, 20\}$ forces two numbers whose sum is exactly 21.

Solution: Construct 10 complementary pair boxes: $\{1,20\}, \{2,19\}, \{3,18\}, \dots, \{10,11\}$. By the Pigeonhole Principle, placing 11 distinct numbers into 10 boxes forces two numbers into the same pair box. Since all numbers are distinct, both members of that box must be chosen, and their sum is $x + (21-x) = 21$.

3.2 Generalized Pigeonhole Principle

When the number of objects $N$ significantly exceeds the number of boxes $m$, the **Generalized Pigeonhole Principle** provides a higher minimum occupancy guarantee based on the ceiling of the average occupancy.

The Ceiling Occupancy Formula

If $N$ objects are assigned to $m$ boxes, at least one box must contain at least:

$$\left\lceil \frac{N}{m} \right\rceil \text{ objects}$$

Where $\lceil x \rceil$ (the ceiling function) denotes the smallest integer greater than or equal to $x$. Equivalently, to guarantee at least $r$ objects in some box, the minimum required total number of objects is $m(r-1) + 1$.

Proof Workshop

Worked Example: Project Group Assignments

Twenty-five students are assigned to six project groups. What is the guaranteed minimum group size that must occur somewhere?

  • Average Calculation: $\mu = 25/6 = 4.166\dots$
  • Ceiling Bound: $\lceil 25/6 \rceil = 5$ students.
  • Contradiction Check: If every group contained at most 4 students, 6 groups could accommodate at most $6 \times 4 = 24$ students, leaving 1 student unassigned!
  • Sharpness Check: The distribution $(5, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4)$ sums to 25, proving we cannot universally guarantee a group of size 6.

Practice Exercise: Birth Weekdays

How many people are required to guarantee that at least 4 share a birth weekday?
Solution: With $m = 7$ weekdays and target $r = 4$, the threshold formula gives $m(r-1) + 1 = 7(4-1) + 1 = 22$ people. With 21 people, having 3 people per weekday avoids a group of 4.

3.3 Average-Value and Weighted Pigeonhole Arguments

The pigeonhole principle is a discrete instance of a broader mathematical truth: In any set of real values, at least one value is greater than or equal to the average, and at least one value is less than or equal to the average. When components have varying sizes or capacities, we use weighted averages.

Weighted Average Formula

Let $r_1, \dots, r_m$ be performance rates assigned positive weights $w_1, \dots, w_m$. The weighted average is:

$$\mu_w = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m} w_i r_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{m} w_i}$$

At least one rate $r_i$ satisfies $r_i \ge \mu_w$, and at least one rate satisfies $r_i \le \mu_w$.

Capacity Overload Proof without Division: If box $i$ has capacity $c_i$ and total objects $N > \sum c_i$, then at least one box must exceed its capacity.

3.4 Infinite Pigeonhole Principle

The Infinite Pigeonhole Principle extends partitioning logic to infinite sets: If an infinite set is partitioned into finitely many subset classes, then at least one class must contain infinitely many elements.

Formal Statement & Proof

Let $S$ be an infinite set partitioned into $m$ disjoint classes $S_1, S_2, \dots, S_m$ (where $m$ is finite). Then at least one class $S_i$ is infinite.

Proof by Contradiction: If every class $S_1, \dots, S_m$ were finite, then their union $S = \bigcup_{i=1}^m S_i$ would be a finite union of finite sets, which is finite—contradicting the premise that $S$ is infinite!

Application to Sequences: In any infinite sequence over a finite alphabet (e.g., $R, B, G$), at least one symbol occurs infinitely often, forming an infinite constant subsequence.

3.5 Introductory Ramsey Theory and Ramsey's Theorem

Pigeonhole arguments force repetition of individual elements. Ramsey Theory asks a deeper structural question: How large must a system be to guarantee a completely monochromatic sub-structure?

For graph edge colorings, a Ramsey number $R(s, t)$ denotes the minimum number of vertices $N$ such that every Red-Blue edge coloring of $K_N$ contains either a solid Red $K_s$ or a solid Blue $K_t$.

3.6 Applications to Sequences, Geometry, and Number Theory

The pigeonhole principle yields profound results across diverse branches of mathematics when applied to clever choice of boxes.

1. Sequences: Consecutive Sub-Block Sum Divisibility

Theorem: Given any sequence of $m$ integers $(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m)$, there is always a non-empty contiguous block whose sum is divisible by $m$.

Proof via Prefix Sums: Define $m+1$ prefix sums $S_0 = 0$ and $S_j = \sum_{\ell=1}^j a_\ell$. Since there are $m+1$ prefix sums and only $m$ remainder classes modulo $m$, two prefix sums $S_i$ and $S_j$ ($i < j$) must share the same remainder modulo $m$. Subtracting them gives:

$$S_j - S_i = a_{i+1} + a_{i+2} + \dots + a_j \equiv 0 \pmod m$$

2. Geometry: Nearby Points in a Unit Square

Theorem: Place 5 points inside a $1 \times 1$ closed unit square. At least two points are separated by a Euclidean distance $d \le 1/\sqrt{2}$.

3. Number Theory: Divisibility in $\{1, 2, \dots, 2n\}$

Theorem: Choose $n+1$ distinct integers from $\{1, 2, \dots, 2n\}$. At least one chosen integer strictly divides another!

Find the Sharp Pigeonhole Bound and Test a Ramsey Coloring

In this investigation, we combine proof diagnosis, sharp threshold constructions, and algorithmic verification to examine the exact boundaries of the Pigeonhole Principle and Ramsey Theory.

Comprehensive Investigation

Investigation: Sharp Bounds & Prefix Remainder Algorithms

Part 1: Sharp Occupancy Bound Proof

Theorem: For $N$ objects and $m$ boxes, the minimum achievable maximum occupancy across all possible distributions is exactly $\lceil N/m \rceil$.

Constructive Proof of Sharpness:

Write $N = qm + r$, where $0 \le r < m$ and $q = \lfloor N/m \rfloor$.

  • Place $q+1$ objects into each of $r$ boxes.
  • Place $q$ objects into each of the remaining $m-r$ boxes.
  • Total objects check: $r(q+1) + (m-r)q = rq + r + mq - rq = mq + r = N$.
  • When $r = 0$, maximum occupancy is $q = \lceil N/m \rceil$. When $r > 0$, maximum occupancy is $q+1 = \lceil N/m \rceil$. Thus, the ceiling bound is universally sharp!

Part 2: Prefix Remainder Divisible-Sum Algorithm

We implement a linear-time algorithm that locates a contiguous sub-block whose sum is divisible by $m$ using prefix sum remainders:

function find_divisible_subblock(arr, m):
    remainder_map = {0: 0}  // Base prefix sum S0 = 0 at index 0
    current_sum = 0

    for j from 1 to length(arr):
        current_sum += arr[j]
        rem = ((current_sum % m) + m) % m  // Non-negative remainder

        if rem in remainder_map:
            i = remainder_map[rem]
            return arr[i+1 ... j]  // Contiguous sub-block
        else:
            remainder_map[rem] = j
