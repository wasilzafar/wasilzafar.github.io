Welcome to Combinatorics: The Art of Reasoning Without Counting Everything

Welcome to the world of Combinatorics—the branch of mathematics dedicated to selecting, arranging, analyzing, and proving properties of discrete structures. At its core, combinatorial thinking addresses four fundamental questions: How many objects exist? Does a solution exist? How do we construct one? And what invariant structure must every valid solution obey?

To launch our journey into combinatorial reasoning, let us begin with a classic puzzle that demonstrates the true magic of this field: ruling out trillions of potential arrangements in a single thought without testing a single one manually.

The Motivating Problem: The Mutilated Chessboard

Imagine covering a standard $8 \times 8$ chessboard with $1 \times 2$ dominoes. Each domino covers exactly two edge-adjacent squares (horizontally or vertically). Dominoes must lie flat, cannot overlap, and cannot hang over the edge of the board.

On an ordinary board with 64 squares, tiling is trivial: simply lay 32 dominoes horizontally across consecutive pairs of squares in each row. But what happens if we alter the geometry?

Suppose we trim away two opposite corners—the upper-left square and the lower-right square—leaving a "mutilated" board with 62 squares. Can we still tile the remaining board using 31 dominoes?

The Naive Calculation (Area Test)

A quick area check shows that $62 \div 2 = 31$ dominoes. The total area is even, so mathematically $31$ dominoes match the total square count. While this confirms a necessary condition, area alone does not guarantee that the geometric pieces will actually fit together!

The Combinatorial Insight (Coloring Invariant)

Observe the underlying structure: every domino must cover exactly 1 black square and 1 white square. A full board has 32 black and 32 white squares. Because opposite corners share the same color (both black), removing them leaves 30 black squares and 32 white squares.

Here lies our elegant verdict: any valid placement of 31 dominoes would inevitably cover 31 black and 31 white squares. Because our mutilated board contains only 30 black squares, a perfect domino tiling is mathematically impossible!

The Essence of Combinatorial Proofs

Instead of laboriously attempting every one of the millions of possible domino arrangements, we identified an invariant property—the 1-to-1 color balance—that every valid tiling must satisfy. Demonstrating that the mutilated board violates this invariant instantly certifies impossibility across all possible configurations.

Motivating Problem

Can a mutilated chessboard be tiled?

Try the same question on a 4-by-4 board. Number rows and columns from 1 to 4, and color a square black when its row number plus column number is even.

  1. Remove squares (1,1) and (4,4). Both are black.
  2. Count the remaining squares: 6 black and 8 white.
  3. Assume a tiling exists. Its seven dominoes would cover seven black and seven white squares.
  4. Compare these requirements with the actual counts. They disagree, so the assumed tiling cannot exist.

Question to revisit: If equal color counts are necessary, are they also sufficient? Keep the two claims separate: a test that rules out some arrangements need not guarantee all the others.

Introductory Examples: Foundational Combinatorial Problems

Combinatorics studies finite or discrete objects and the ways they can be selected, arranged, connected, or constrained. Four recurring core questions guide our thinking:

  1. How many objects exist? (Counting & Enumeration)
  2. Does an object exist? (Existence & Impossibility Proofs)
  3. How can we construct one? (Algorithms & Explicit Constructions)
  4. What structure must every object have? (Invariants & Structural Theorems)

To build our combinatorial intuition, let us explore seven classic foundational examples that showcase these core questions in action.

Example 1: Perfect Covers of Chessboards

A perfect cover (or domino tiling) of a board with $1 \times 2$ dominoes is an arrangement where every square is covered by exactly one domino without any overlaps or spills over the boundary.

For an $m \times n$ rectangular board to have a perfect cover, an obvious necessary condition is that the total area $m \cdot n$ must be an even number. If $m \cdot n$ is odd, no tiling can exist because each domino covers exactly 2 squares.

Example 2: Magic Squares

A magic square of order $n$ is an $n \times n$ grid containing the integers $1, 2, \dots, n^2$ such that the numbers in each row, each column, and both main diagonals add up to the exact same sum, called the magic constant $M$.

The magic constant for an $n \times n$ square is given by the formula:

$$M = \frac{n(n^2 + 1)}{2}$$

For $n = 3$, the total sum of numbers $1$ through $9$ is $45$. Dividing equally across $3$ rows gives $M = \frac{3(3^2+1)}{2} = 15$.

Example 3: The Four-Color Problem

Proposed by Francis Guthrie in 1852, the Four-Color Problem asks: Can any planar map (a division of the plane into contiguous regions) be colored using at most four colors such that no two adjacent regions sharing a border segment have the same color?

While intuitive to state, it proved extraordinarily resistant to proof. In 1976, Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken proved the Four-Color Theorem using a pioneering combination of mathematical reduction and computer-assisted verification of 1,936 reducible configuration cases.

Example 4: The Problem of the 36 Officers

In 1779, Leonhard Euler posed his famous 36 Officers Problem: Suppose 36 officers belong to 6 different regiments, with 6 officers of different ranks in each regiment. Can they be arranged in a $6 \times 6$ square grid such that every row and every column contains exactly one officer of each rank and one officer of each regiment?

Mathematically, this seeks two Orthogonal Latin Squares (or a Graeco-Latin Square) of order $n = 6$. While solutions exist for $n = 3, 4, 5$, Euler conjectured that no solution exists for any $n \equiv 2 \pmod 4$ (i.e., $n = 2, 6, 10, \dots$). In 1901, French mathematician Gaston Tarry proved by exhaustive case analysis that Euler was right for $n = 6$: the 36 Officers Problem is mathematically impossible!

Example 5: Shortest-Route Problem

On a rectangular grid, consider traveling from the origin $(0,0)$ to a target point $(m,n)$ along grid edges, taking only steps to the East ($E$) or North ($N$).

Every shortest route consists of exactly $m$ East steps and $n$ North steps, for a total route length of $m+n$ steps. We can bijectively encode each route as a word of length $m+n$ containing exactly $m$ '$E$'s and $n$ '$N$'s. Choosing $n$ North step positions out of $m+n$ total positions gives the total route count:

$$\text{Total Routes} = \binom{m+n}{n} = \frac{(m+n)!}{m! \, n!}$$

Example 6: Mutually Overlapping Circles

Suppose $n$ circles are drawn in a plane in general position (meaning every pair of circles intersects at exactly two points, and no three circles share a common point of intersection). Into how many distinct regions do these $n$ circles divide the plane?

Adding the $n$-th circle intersects the previous $n-1$ circles at $2(n-1)$ points. These points split the new circle into $2(n-1)$ arcs, each of which divides an existing region into two. Thus, $R(n) = R(n-1) + 2(n-1)$, leading to the recurrence solution:

$$R(n) = n^2 - n + 2$$

For $n = 3$ circles, $R(3) = 3^2 - 3 + 2 = 8$ regions (including the single unbounded exterior region).

Example 7: The Game of Nim

In the classic mathematical game of Nim, players take turns removing any number of counters from a single heap. Under normal play rules, the player to take the final counter wins.

In 1901, Charles L. Bouton completely solved Nim using the binary bitwise XOR operation, now called the Nim-sum ($\oplus$). A position with heap sizes $(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k)$ is a losing position (P-position) for the current player if and only if:

$$x_1 \oplus x_2 \oplus \dots \oplus x_k = 0$$

If the Nim-sum is non-zero, the current player can always make a move that leaves a zero Nim-sum for the opponent, securing a guaranteed winning position (N-position)!

Before counting or proving anything, specify the objects, the constraints, and what makes two outcomes different. A route count, a tiling impossibility proof, and a winning strategy answer different questions, even when all use discrete reasoning.

1.2 Fundamental Counting Rules: Addition, Multiplication, Subtraction, & Division

At the heart of combinatorics lies set cardinality. For any finite set $A$, the notation $|A|$ represents the exact number of elements in $A$. To count complex structures accurately without missing items or double-counting, we rely on four fundamental counting operations.

Rule 1

The Addition Rule (Rule of Sum)

When choosing an object from one of several distinct categories, the total number of choices depends on whether those categories share any common elements.

Disjoint Categories (No Overlap)

If sets $A$ and $B$ are disjoint ($A \cap B = \emptyset$), simply add their sizes:

$$|A \cup B| = |A| + |B|$$

Example: Choosing 1 drink from 3 teas or 2 coffees (where no drink is both) gives $3 + 2 = 5$ choices.

Overlapping Categories (Inclusion-Exclusion)

If categories overlap, simple addition double-counts shared elements ($A \cap B$). Subtract the intersection to correct it:

$$|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|$$

Example: If 8 study chess, 7 study coding, and 3 study both, then $8 + 7 - 3 = 12$ study at least one.

Rule 2

The Multiplication Rule (Rule of Product)

When a procedure breaks down into a sequence of independent stages, multiply the number of choices available at each step.

Multi-Stage Sequential Choice Formula

If step 1 has $a$ possible outcomes and step 2 has $b$ possible outcomes (regardless of which choice was made in step 1), the total number of combined outcomes is:

$$\text{Total Outcomes} = a \times b$$

Example: Choosing 1 shirt from 3 available options and 1 pair of trousers from 2 available options yields $3 \times 2 = 6$ distinct outfit combinations.

Note on Dependent Choices: The available choices in the second step may depend on the first choice, provided the number of choices available at step 2 remains constantly equal to $b$. If the count of second-stage options varies based on step 1, split the problem into disjoint branches and apply the Addition Rule across the branches.
Rule 3

The Subtraction Rule (Complementary Counting)

When directly counting valid outcomes is tedious or complex, count the entire universe of possibilities first and subtract the invalid ones.

Complementary Counting Formula

If $B$ represents the subset of invalid outcomes inside a universal set $A$ ($B \subseteq A$), the number of valid outcomes $|A \setminus B|$ is:

$$|A \setminus B| = |A| - |B|$$

Example: How many 3-bit binary strings contain at least one 1?

  • Total possible 3-bit strings ($|A|$): $2^3 = 8$ total strings.
  • Forbidden strings with no 1s ($|B|$): Exactly 1 string (000).
  • Valid strings ($|A \setminus B|$): $8 - 1 = 7$ qualifying strings.
Rule 4

The Division Rule (Overcounting Correction)

When an initial counting strategy treats distinct representations of the same underlying object as separate outcomes, divide by the uniform repetition factor $k$.

Symmetry Correction Formula

If every target object is represented exactly $k$ times in a set of $n$ ordered encodings, the total number of distinct target objects is:

$$\text{Target Objects} = \frac{n}{k}$$

Example: Choosing an unordered 2-person committee from a group of 5 people.

  1. Count ordered pairs ($n$): The first member can be chosen in 5 ways; the second in 4 ways ($5 \times 4 = 20$ ordered pairs).
  2. Identify overcounting multiplicity ($k$): Each committee $\{P_1, P_2\}$ is counted twice (as $(P_1, P_2)$ and $(P_2, P_1)$), so $k = 2$.
  3. Apply Division Rule: $\frac{20}{2} = 10$ distinct committees.
Crucial Requirement: Division is valid only when every single target object has the exact same multiplicity $k$. Never divide by a symmetry count unless constant multiplicity across all objects is strictly verified.

Quick Reference: Selecting the Right Counting Rule

Counting Rule Key Condition Mathematical Formula When to Use
Addition Disjoint choices or overlapping sets $|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|$ Choosing 1 item from distinct/OR categories.
Multiplication Sequential independent steps $\text{Total} = a \times b$ Building multi-stage AND combinations.
Subtraction Subset $B \subseteq A$ of invalid cases $|A \setminus B| = |A| - |B|$ Direct count is harder than "Total − Forbidden".

Proof Workshop

Addition, multiplication, subtraction, and division rules: a worked example

Five students must form a two-person committee. How many committees contain at least one of two designated mentors, A and B?

Route 1: total minus forbidden. There are $5 \times 4 / 2 = 10$ committees in total. A forbidden committee chooses both members from the other three students, giving $3 \times 2 / 2 = 3$. The answer is $10 - 3 = 7$.

Route 2: disjoint cases. A committee contains either exactly one mentor or both mentors. Exactly one mentor gives $2 \times 3 = 6$ choices: choose the mentor and then the nonmentor. Both mentors give the single committee {A,B}. Addition gives $6 + 1 = 7$.

Assumption check. Members are distinct, committee order does not matter, the two cases cannot overlap, and every qualifying committee belongs to one case.

Practice. Repeat with six students, still including exactly two designated mentors.

Hint and answer. Exactly one mentor gives $2 \times 4 = 8$ committees; both gives one. The answer is nine. Independently, $6 \times 5 / 2 - 4 \times 3 / 2 = 15 - 6 = 9$.

1.3 Bijections & Complementary Counting

A bijection is a reversible pairing between two sets: every object in the first set corresponds to exactly one object in the second, and every object in the second has exactly one partner. A bijection proves that the two finite sets have equal sizes.

Consider subsets of $\{1,2,3\}$ and binary strings of length three. Given a subset $S$, write a 1 in position $i$ precisely when $i$ belongs to $S$. For example, $\{1,3\}$ becomes $101$.

The inverse operation reads a string and collects the positions containing 1. Thus $101$ becomes $\{1,3\}$. Applying either operation and then its inverse returns the starting object. This verifies reversibility; merely displaying a few matching examples would not establish it in general.

Every position has two choices, so there are $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$ strings and therefore eight subsets. For a set with $n$ elements, the same construction gives $2^n$ subsets, including the empty subset.

Complementary counting counts a desired collection by removing its opposite inside a clearly defined universe. Of the eight subsets here, only the empty subset has no elements, so seven are nonempty. Through the bijection, this is the same statement as: seven three-bit strings contain at least one 1.

Exercise. How many subsets of $\{1,2,3,4\}$ contain 1?

Hint and answer. The first bit is fixed at 1; the remaining three bits are free. There are eight such subsets. Equivalently, add 1 to each subset of $\{2,3,4\}$, a reversible construction.

1.4 Double Counting and Counting in Two Ways

Double counting (or counting in two ways) is one of the most elegant proof techniques in combinatorics. By identifying a single set of objects or relationships and evaluating its size from two completely different perspectives, we produce a rigorous algebraic equality without performing tedious calculations.

Concept 1

Counting Incidences: The Handshake Problem

Suppose 6 people attend a gathering and every pair shakes hands exactly once. How many total handshakes occur?

Rather than counting handshakes directly, define a two-element relationship called an incidence: a pair $(p, h)$ consisting of a person $p$ and a handshake $h$ in which person $p$ participated.

Perspective A: Person-Centric Count

Each of the 6 people shakes hands with the other 5 attendees. The total number of person–handshake incidences is:

$$\text{Incidences} = 6 \times 5 = 30$$
Perspective B: Handshake-Centric Count

Let $h$ be the total number of handshakes. Because every handshake involves exactly 2 participants, each handshake creates 2 incidences:

$$\text{Incidences} = 2 \times h$$
Equating Both Perspectives

Because both perspectives count the exact same set of incidences, the two expressions must be equal:

$$2h = 30 \implies h = 15 \text{ handshakes}$$
Concept 2

Generalization: The Handshake Lemma on Graphs

This principle extends directly to graph theory. A simple undirected graph $G = (V, E)$ consists of a set of $V$ vertices and $E$ edges joining distinct pairs of vertices.

The degree of a vertex $v$, written $\deg(v)$, is the number of edges connected to $v$. Counting vertex–edge incidences in two ways yields one of graph theory's foundational results:

The Handshake Lemma (Degree Sum Formula)

In any finite undirected graph $G = (V, E)$, the sum of the degrees of all vertices is equal to twice the total number of edges:

$$\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2|E|$$

Let us verify this formula on two basic graph structures:

  • Triangle Graph ($K_3$): 3 vertices, each with degree 2. Degree sum $= 2 + 2 + 2 = 6$. Number of edges $= 3$, and $2 \times 3 = 6$.
  • Path Graph ($P_4$): 4 vertices with degrees $1, 2, 2, 1$. Degree sum $= 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 6$. Number of edges $= 3$, and $2 \times 3 = 6$.
Concept 3

The Odd-Degree Parity Theorem

A immediate structural corollary of the Handshake Lemma governs the distribution of odd-degree vertices across any network.

The Parity Theorem

In any undirected graph, the number of vertices with an odd degree must always be EVEN.

Proof Workshop

Exercise: Can a graph have exactly 3 odd-degree vertices?

Suppose someone claims to have built a simple undirected network with exactly three vertices of odd degree. Prove whether this claim is mathematically possible.

Proof Breakdown:

  1. Partition the vertex set $V$ into two subsets: $V_{\text{even}}$ (vertices with even degrees) and $V_{\text{odd}}$ (vertices with odd degrees).
  2. Express the degree sum formula as: $$\sum_{v \in V_{\text{even}}} \deg(v) + \sum_{v \in V_{\text{odd}}} \deg(v) = 2|E|$$
  3. The right side $2|E|$ is always an even integer.
  4. The sum of even degrees $\sum_{v \in V_{\text{even}}} \deg(v)$ is a sum of even numbers, which is always even.
  5. Subtracting even from even requires $\sum_{v \in V_{\text{odd}}} \deg(v)$ to be even.
  6. A sum of odd integers equals an even number if and only if there is an even count of odd terms.

Conclusion: $|V_{\text{odd}}|$ must be even. Therefore, having exactly 3 odd-degree vertices is IMPOSSIBLE!

Double Counting Applications at a Glance

1.5 Invariants and Monovariants

When analyzing dynamical systems, games, or recursive algorithms, tracking every possible sequence of states quickly becomes impossible. Instead, we analyze global properties that govern state transitions: invariants (quantities that never change) and monovariants (quantities that strictly move in one direction).

Concept 1

Invariants: Proving Unreachability

An invariant is a property or numerical value that remains completely unchanged under every legal transition or move. Invariance is process-dependent: a quantity preserved under one rule set may change under another.

Example: The 2-Coin Flip Game

A row contains 7 coins, initially all showing Tails ($0$ Heads). A legal move consists of selecting any 2 distinct coins and flipping both simultaneously. Can we ever reach a state with exactly 1 Head?

Let $H$ represent the total number of Heads. Inspect all three possible flip outcomes:

Outcome 1
Flip 2 Tails

Both become Heads.
$$\Delta H = +2$$

Outcome 2
Flip 2 Heads

Both become Tails.
$$\Delta H = -2$$

Outcome 3
Flip 1 Head + 1 Tail

Head $\to$ Tail, Tail $\to$ Head.
$$\Delta H = 0$$

In every single scenario, $H$ changes by an even amount ($\pm 2$ or $0$). Therefore, the parity of $H$ (whether $H$ is even or odd) is an invariant. Because we start with $H = 0$ (even), $H$ must remain even forever. A target state with $H = 1$ (odd) can never be reached.

Concept 2

Monovariants: Proving Termination

While an invariant stays completely fixed, a monovariant is a value that changes in only one direction (strictly increasing or strictly decreasing). A strictly decreasing quantity bounded below by zero guarantees that a process must eventually terminate.

Example: Subtraction Euclidean Algorithm

Start with two positive integers $(a, b)$. If $a \neq b$, replace the larger number with the difference between them. Repeat until $a = b$.

Trace for (18, 12):   (18, 12) ➔ (6, 12) ➔ (6, 6) [Terminal]

Answering two fundamental questions about this algorithm requires two distinct tools:

1. Monovariant (Proves Termination)

Consider the sum $S = a + b$. At each step, replacing $\max(a,b)$ with $|a - b|$ strictly reduces the sum $S$ by $2 \min(a,b) > 0$.

$$S_{\text{next}} < S_{\text{current}}$$

Since $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}^+$, $S$ is a positive integer that cannot decrease indefinitely. Therefore, the process must stop.

2. Invariant (Proves Correctness)

Consider $\gcd(a, b)$. Number theory establishes that any divisor of $a$ and $b$ also divides $a - b$. Thus, the greatest common divisor is preserved:

$$\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(\min(a,b), |a - b|)$$

At terminal state $(d, d)$, $\gcd(d, d) = d$. Invariance guarantees $d = \gcd(a_{\text{initial}}, b_{\text{initial}})$.

Hands-On Practice

Exercise: Apply the Monovariant & Invariant Analysis

Apply the subtraction Euclidean algorithm to the starting pair $(15, 9)$.

  1. List the complete step-by-step state sequence until a terminal state is reached.
  2. Identify the exact monovariant values (sum of pairs) at each step.
  3. Verify the invariant value ($\gcd$) across all states.

Solution & Trace Breakdown:

State Trace: (15, 9) ➔ (6, 9) ➔ (6, 3) ➔ (3, 3)

  • Monovariant Sum Sequence: $24 \to 15 \to 9 \to 6$ (strictly decreasing positive integers $\implies$ guarantees termination).
  • Invariant GCD Value: $\gcd(15, 9) = \gcd(6, 9) = \gcd(6, 3) = \gcd(3, 3) = 3$ (strictly preserved $\implies$ guarantees correct output $3$).

Invariants vs. Monovariants: Side-by-Side Comparison

Property Behavior During Transitions Primary Mathematical Purpose Typical Examples
Invariant Stays 100% constant ($V_{\text{next}} = V_{\text{current}}$) Proves unreachability or correctness Parity, color balance, $\gcd(a,b)$, degree sum.
Monovariant Changes in one direction ($V_{\text{next}} < V_{\text{current}}$) Proves termination / finiteness Sum of entries, total energy, distance to target.

1.6 Parity, Coloring, and Impossibility Arguments

When facing complex combinatorial problems with millions of possibilities, testing every configuration directly is rarely feasible. Instead, we simplify the problem by extracting a single invariant property—a feature that stays constant across all valid states. Two of the most versatile tools for certifying impossibility are parity and coloring.

Concept 1

Parity & Area-Parity Obstructions

Parity simply records whether an integer is even or odd. By collapsing numbers down to this single bit of information ($0$ or $1$), complex geometric constraints often reduce to simple arithmetic rules.

The Area-Parity Rule for Domino Tilings

Every $1 \times 2$ domino covers exactly 2 squares (an even number). Therefore, any region formed by combining $k$ dominoes must have a total area of $2k$ squares (always an even integer). A region with an odd number of available squares can never be tiled by dominoes.

Concept 2

Coloring & Weighted-Sum Invariants

Area parity alone cannot catch every impossibility. For instance, removing two opposite corner squares from an $8 \times 8$ board leaves 62 squares—an even area! To detect why this mutilated board cannot be tiled, we introduce checkerboard coloring.

Assign numerical weights to the squares based on their colors:

  • Assign every black square a weight of $+1$.
  • Assign every white square a weight of $-1$.

Because every domino covers exactly 1 black and 1 white square, the weight sum of a single domino is always $(+1) + (-1) = 0$. Consequently, any region that can be perfectly tiled by dominoes must have a total weight sum of $0$.

On the $4 \times 4$ mutilated board with black corner squares $(1,1)$ and $(4,4)$ removed, the total weight is $6(+1) + 8(-1) = -2$. Since no sum of zero-weight dominoes can ever equal $-2$, the board cannot be tiled.

Concept 3

Necessary vs. Sufficient Conditions (Local Obstructions)

A common pitfall in mathematical reasoning is assuming that if a proposed solution passes a necessary test, it must be valid. A necessary condition is not automatically sufficient.

Necessary Condition

A requirement that must be satisfied for a tiling to exist (e.g., equal numbers of black and white squares, even area). If it fails, nonexistence is certified.

Sufficient Condition

A condition that guarantees a tiling exists. Passing color balance alone is not sufficient because local structural bottlenecks can still prevent tiling!

Counterexample: The Isolated Square

Consider a $4 \times 4$ board where four specific squares are removed: $(1,2)$, $(2,1)$, $(3,3)$, and $(4,4)$.

  • Removed white squares: $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$ (2 white).
  • Removed black squares: $(3,3)$ and $(4,4)$ (2 black).

The board has 6 black squares and 6 white squares remaining. Color balance is perfectly preserved ($6 = 6$). However, inspect corner square $(1,1)$:

Square $(1,1)$ requires at least one available adjacent square to place a domino. Because both of its edge-sharing neighbors—$(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$—were removed, square $(1,1)$ is completely isolated. No domino can ever cover it, making tiling impossible despite perfect $6 = 6$ color balance.

Summary: Impossibility Arguments at a Glance

Technique Invariant Checked When It Certifies Impossibility Scope & Limitation
Area Parity Total Square Count ($\text{Area} \bmod 2$) Total available area is an odd integer. Global test. Cannot detect color or shape imbalances.
Checkerboard Coloring Weighted Sum $\sum W_i = \#\text{Black} - \#\text{White}$ Count of black squares $\neq$ count of white squares. Global invariant. Necessary test, but not sufficient.
Local Connectivity Vertex Degree / Available Neighbors Any isolated square has $0$ available neighbors. Local test. Catches bottlenecks that global tests miss.

1.7 Existence Proofs versus Constructive Proofs

In combinatorics, proving that an object or configuration exists can be approached in two fundamentally different ways. A constructive proof provides an explicit algorithm, formula, or visual blueprint for building the object. In contrast, an existence proof (often non-constructive) mathematically guarantees that at least one such object must exist—frequently via bounds, contradiction, or the Pigeonhole Principle—without revealing where or how to find it.

Method 1

Constructive Proofs: Explicit Recipes

A constructive proof answers both "Does it exist?" and "How do we build it?" simultaneously. It specifies an exact step-by-step procedure to construct a valid instance for any input parameter.

Constructive Example: Domino Tiling of Even-Column Rectangles

Theorem: Any $m \times n$ rectangular board where the number of columns $n$ is even can be perfectly tiled with $1 \times 2$ dominoes.

Explicit Construction Algorithm:

  1. Iterate through each row $r$ from $1$ to $m$.
  2. Within row $r$, pair column $1$ with $2$, column $3$ with $4$, and generally column $2k-1$ with column $2k$ for all $k \in \{1, 2, \dots, n/2\}$.
  3. Place a horizontal $1 \times 2$ domino across each paired set of adjacent squares.

Because $n$ is even, every square belongs to exactly one pair, no dominoes overlap, and no dominoes extend past the boundary. This explicit recipe completes the proof by direct demonstration.

Method 2

Non-Constructive Existence Proofs: Proving Without Showing

A non-constructive existence proof proves that an object must exist beyond any mathematical doubt, yet it leaves the exact identity or location of that object unspecified. These proofs typically rely on bounding arguments, counting contradictions, or the Pigeonhole Principle.

Existence Example: The Server Workload Bound

Theorem: If 20 tasks are assigned among 6 cloud servers, at least one server must be assigned 4 or more tasks.

Proof by Contradiction (Bounding Argument):

  1. Assume the contrary: suppose every server is assigned at most $3$ tasks.
  2. With $6$ servers each handling at most $3$ tasks, the maximum possible total number of tasks that can be processed is: $$\text{Max Tasks} = 6 \times 3 = 18 \text{ tasks}$$
  3. However, we have $20$ tasks in total. Since $18 < 20$, this contradicts our initial assumption!
  4. Therefore, at least one server must carry $\ge 4$ tasks.
Key Distinction: Notice that this proof guarantees the existence of a heavily loaded server without telling us which server it is (e.g., Server 2 vs. Server 5) or how the tasks were distributed!

Constructive vs. Non-Constructive Proofs: At a Glance

1.8 Introduction to Extremal Reasoning

When investigating discrete systems, one of the most powerful problem-solving strategies is extremal reasoning. Instead of examining an arbitrary or average configuration, we select an extremal object—an element that maximizes or minimizes a key measurement (such as longest path, smallest angle, maximum degree, or minimum distance). Because an extremal object sits at an absolute structural limit, it cannot be further improved, forcing local properties that simplify proofs.

Concept 1

The Extremal Principle: Maximums & Minimums

An extremal choice converts a global assumption into tight local structural restrictions. The core logic follows a classic proof-by-contradiction pattern:

Standard Extremal Strategy
  1. Identify a measurable quantity $f(x)$ for every valid candidate object $x$.
  2. Select an object $x^*$ that achieves the absolute maximum (or minimum) value of $f$.
  3. Exploit the fact that $x^*$ cannot be enlarged/extended: any operation that would increase $f(x^*)$ must be impossible, revealing mandatory structural constraints on $x^*$.
Concept 2

Worked Proof: Longest Paths Reveal Local Structure

Let $G = (V, E)$ be a finite simple undirected graph. A path is a sequence of distinct vertices $v_0, v_1, \dots, v_k$ where consecutive vertices are connected by an edge. Its length is its number of edges ($k$).

Key Theorem: Neighbor Containment

Choose a longest path $P = (v_0, v_1, \dots, v_k)$ in $G$. Then every neighbor of the starting vertex $v_0$ must already lie on $P$.

Proof by Contradiction:

Suppose $v_0$ had a neighbor $w$ that does not belong to path $P$. Because $w \notin \{v_0, v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ and $(w, v_0) \in E$, we could append $w$ to the front of $P$ to form a new path:

$$P' = (w, v_0, v_1, \dots, v_k) \quad \implies \quad \text{Length}(P') = k + 1$$

Path $P'$ has length $k + 1$, directly contradicting our initial premise that $P$ was a longest path (length $k$). Thus, no such external neighbor $w$ can exist!

Concept 3

Application: Minimum Degree Bounds Guarantee Long Paths

Now consider how this extremal property lets us prove a fundamental graph theory theorem connecting vertex degrees to path lengths.

Theorem: Minimum Degree $\delta$ Guarantees Path of Length $\ge \delta$

Suppose every vertex in a non-empty finite graph $G$ has degree at least $\delta$ ($\deg(v) \ge \delta$ for all $v \in V$). Then $G$ contains a path of length at least $\delta$ (containing at least $\delta + 1$ vertices).

Step-by-Step Proof Using Extremal Reasoning:

  1. Select a longest path $P = (v_0, v_1, \dots, v_k)$ in graph $G$.
  2. Inspect starting vertex $v_0$. By hypothesis, $\deg(v_0) \ge \delta$, so $v_0$ has at least $\delta$ distinct neighbors.
  3. By our extremal neighbor-containment theorem, all $\delta$ neighbors of $v_0$ must lie on path $P$.
  4. Since $v_0$ cannot be its own neighbor, all $\ge \delta$ neighbors must be chosen from the remaining path vertices $\{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k\}$.
  5. Because $P$ has $k$ vertices other than $v_0$, the total number of remaining path vertices must be at least $\delta$: $$\text{Path length } k \ge \delta$$

Extremal Reasoning Patterns at a Glance

Extremal Choice Impossibility / Barrier Structural Consequence Revealed
Longest Path $P = (v_0, \dots, v_k)$ Cannot add an external vertex to extend $P$. All neighbors of $v_0$ (and $v_k$) lie on $P$.
Smallest Geometry Distance Cannot fit another point inside the minimal gap. Forces points to lie on boundaries or collinear configurations.
Maximum Degree Vertex $v_{\max}$ No vertex has strictly more incident edges than $v_{\max}$. Upper-bounds neighbor set sizes across the entire graph.

Design A Counting Proof and An Impossibility Certificate

The investigation below combines the chapter's main habits: define objects precisely, count without duplication, construct legal outcomes, and certify impossible ones. It also introduces a useful distinction between checking examples computationally and proving a statement for every input.

Investigation

Design a counting proof and an impossibility certificate

Inputs and conventions. Use rectangles with rows and columns numbered from 1. A domino covers two edge-adjacent available squares. Dominoes are indistinguishable, and two tilings are different when they use different pairs of squares. Rotated arrangements are counted separately when their square pairs differ.

Warm-up calculations.

  1. Count the shortest routes from $(0,0)$ to $(3,2)$, showing the reversible encoding by words. Expected answer: ten.
  2. Count the nonempty subsets of $\{1,2,3,4\}$. Expected answer: $2^4 - 1 = 15$.
  3. Count two-person committees from six people. Expected answer: $6 \times 5 / 2 = 15$; explain why every committee has exactly two ordered encodings.

Counting workshop: domino tilings of a 2-by-n rectangle. Let $T(n)$ count its tilings. Set $T(0)=1$: the empty board has the one empty tiling. Also $T(1)=1$.

For $n \ge 2$, inspect the upper-left square. If its domino is vertical, remove the first column, leaving a 2-by-$(n-1)$ rectangle. If its domino is horizontal, the lower-left square must also be covered horizontally: its upper neighbor is already occupied, and its only other available neighbor is to the right. Removing these two horizontal dominoes leaves a 2-by-$(n-2)$ rectangle.

These two cases are disjoint and exhaustive, and deleting or adding their initial pieces is reversible. Thus $T(n) = T(n-1) + T(n-2)$. The first values, from $n=0$ through $n=4$, are $1,1,2,3,5$. Draw all three tilings of a 2-by-3 rectangle and check that each belongs to exactly one case.

Deliverables. Submit the three warm-up solutions, drawings for the 2-by-3 board, the recurrence proof, the rectangle construction, the color certificate, and a short record of any computational experiment.
