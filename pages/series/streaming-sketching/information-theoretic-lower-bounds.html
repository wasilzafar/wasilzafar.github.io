How Many Worlds Must a Summary Distinguish?

Suppose a museum has one of eight possible floor plans, but a guide may send you only a two-bit note before you enter. Two bits have four possible messages, so at least two plans receive the same note. If your task requires identifying the exact plan, no decoding trick can repair that collision. This is the counting intuition behind an information-theoretic lower bound: a small state cannot reliably distinguish too many possible worlds.

Streaming sketches replace floor plans with streams and the note with memory. If a hidden label $V$ selects one of $M$ carefully separated data-generating worlds, an accurate estimate may reveal which world generated the stream. The sketch state must therefore retain enough information about $V$. An $s$-bit state has entropy at most $s$, while Fano’s inequality says reliable identification among many labels requires mutual information on the order of $\log_2 M$.

For a tiny example, let $V$ be uniform on four labels. Then $H(V)=2$ bits. A one-bit deterministic summary has only two states and cannot identify all four labels exactly. Randomization can redistribute errors, but it cannot create information: if the observation distributions are too similar or the state is too small, a nontrivial error remains unavoidable.

Two kinds of scarcity must be separated. Memory scarcity limits how much an algorithm retains from observations. Sample scarcity limits how distinguishable the underlying distributions are even with unlimited memory. Some lower bounds combine both, but the proof must say which bottleneck is responsible.

An accurate summary must preserve enough information to separate every hard world that accuracy would distinguish. Entropy counts the hidden uncertainty; mutual information measures how much the observed or stored state removes—not how many bytes happen to be allocated.

Warm-up

How Many Worlds Must a Summary Distinguish?

Let $V$ be uniform on eight labels and suppose the summary has only two bits.

  1. Compute $H(V)=\log_2 8=3$ bits.
  2. A two-bit state has at most four values. Under deterministic encoding, at least two labels share a state.
  3. If labels are evenly paired, a decoder seeing only the state chooses correctly for at most one label in each pair, so average success is at most $4/8=1/2$.
  4. If one extra bit is allowed, eight distinct codewords exist and exact identification becomes possible in this noiseless toy model.

Reflection: why does this counting argument need the task to distinguish all eight labels rather than merely estimate a property shared by several labels?

Entropy, Encodings and Indistinguishable Streams

For a discrete variable $V$ with mass function $p(v)$,

$$ H(V)=-\sum_v p(v)\log_2 p(v). $$

Conditional entropy $H(V\mid S)$ is the residual uncertainty after observing $S$, and mutual information is $I(V;S)=H(V)-H(V\mid S)$. If $S$ takes at most $2^s$ values, then $H(S)\le s$ and therefore $I(V;S)\le s$. This is the memory bottleneck.

Now let $V$ choose a hard distribution $P_V$ over streams $X$. A randomized streaming algorithm maps $X$ and independent coins $R$ to state $S$. The causal chain is $V\to X\to S\to\widehat V$. Data processing gives

$$ I(V;\widehat V)\le I(V;S)\le I(V;X). $$

The first upper bound can also be capped by $s$. The left side is bounded below when decoding error is small. One common Fano form for uniform $V$ on $M$ labels is

$$ P_e\ge 1-\frac{I(V;S)+1}{\log_2 M}. $$

Equivalently, error $P_e\le\delta$ requires $I(V;S)\ge(1-\delta)\log_2 M-1$. Constants vary with logarithm base and theorem form; the quantifiers and distribution must remain explicit.

Statistical indistinguishability uses distances or divergences between observation laws. Total variation controls the best binary testing advantage. Kullback–Leibler divergence and related inequalities can upper-bound distinguishability, often tensorizing across independent samples. Small KL does not mean pointwise likelihoods are equal, and large parameter distance does not guarantee observable distributions are easy to distinguish.

Information cannot increase along the chain from hidden world to observations to bounded state to estimate. If accuracy would decode one of $M$ separated worlds with small error, Fano forces the state to contain roughly $\log_2 M$ bits of information; an $s$-bit state cannot supply more than $s$.

Packing, Testing and Information Arguments

A packing converts estimation into decoding. Choose parameters $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_M$ whose pairwise loss distance exceeds $2\varepsilon$. If an estimator is within $\varepsilon$ of the true parameter, nearest-neighbor decoding recovers its label. Thus an estimator with small risk would induce a successful $M$-way test.

The hard family must balance two competing goals: parameter points must be far apart under the target loss, while their observation distributions remain close under KL, total variation, Hellinger distance, or another divergence. If distributions are too far apart, abundant statistical evidence makes testing easy. If parameters are too close, an inaccurate estimator need not distinguish them.

For memory lower bounds, let the full stream contain ample evidence but force that evidence through an $s$-bit state. For sample lower bounds, bound $I(V;X^n)$ itself. With conditionally independent samples, KL often grows linearly in $n$, so a per-sample information bound becomes an $n$ requirement. For combined bounds, use both $I(V;S)\le s$ and $I(V;S)\le I(V;X^n)$.

Binary testing tools such as Le Cam’s method compare two worlds; Assouad’s lemma embeds many binary coordinates; Fano handles a multiclass packing. Yao’s principle or a Bayes-prior argument can convert distributional hardness into a minimax lower bound, but average risk under a prior is not automatically a worst-case statement without that quantifier step.

Worked Derivation

Packing, Testing and Information Arguments

Let $V$ be uniform over $M=16$ pairwise-separated parameters. Any estimate with loss at most $\varepsilon$ identifies $V$. Suppose the decoder’s error is at most $\delta=1/4$.

  1. The hidden entropy is $H(V)=\log_2 16=4$ bits.
  2. Fano gives $I(V;S)\ge(1-\delta)\log_2 M-1=(3/4)(4)-1=2$ bits.
  3. If the summary state has $s$ bits, then $I(V;S)\le H(S)\le s$.
  4. Therefore $s\ge2$ bits under this coarse Fano form. Sharper counting may prove more in a noiseless setting, but this derivation is valid under randomized error.
  5. If independent observations satisfy an information upper bound $I(V;X^n)\le0.1n$, data processing and Fano also require $0.1n\ge2$, hence $n\ge20$.

Assumption-breaking variation: if all $16$ parameters lie within one $\varepsilon$-ball, an accurate estimate need not reveal $V$. The Fano calculation remains algebraically true for decoding, but estimation no longer supplies a decoder.

Limits of Estimation Under Tiny Memory

In an approachable sensor example, one of many calibrated regimes generates readings. If regimes are well separated in the reported mean but noisy observations are close, samples limit identification. If the readings reveal the regime but a device compresses them to a tiny state before a later query, memory limits identification.

In high-dimensional telemetry, a service may want a sparse histogram, covariance structure, or anomaly direction. A packing can encode exponentially many candidate directions, so $\log M$ scales with dimension. Yet a theorem for Gaussian samples does not automatically apply to heavy-tailed, dependent, or adversarial streams.

In federated learning, each client reveals a privatized or compressed message. Data processing limits information per client, and privacy may further contract distinguishability. But security and privacy are not synonymous with low mutual information under every prior; the channel, adversary knowledge, and composition across rounds must be modeled.

A misleading claim often treats high entropy of raw data as proof that a task needs high memory. That is false when the target is simple: a billion fair coin flips have enormous entropy, but their parity needs one bit and their approximate mean needs little memory. The packing must concern distinctions required by the stated loss.

Case Study

Limits of Estimation Under Tiny Memory: Information-Theoretic & Statistical Lower Bounds

A weather station operates in one of eight calibration regimes, chosen uniformly. A diagnostic stream is rich enough that a full-data analyst can identify the regime, but the device retains only a two-bit state.

  1. The regime label has entropy $3$ bits.
  2. The state has at most four values and at most $2$ bits of entropy.
  3. Under balanced deterministic collisions, at least two regimes share each state, so exact average identification error is at least $1/2$.
  4. If the operational task only asks whether the regime is in $\{1,2,3,4\}$ or $\{5,6,7,8\}$, one bit can suffice. The task loss, not raw regime entropy, controls the required distinctions.

Success criteria: compute the entropy/state counts, distinguish regime identification from group classification, and explain whether the limitation is caused by samples or retained memory.

Production

Limits of Estimation Under Tiny Memory: Information-Theoretic & Statistical Lower Bounds

A fleet of $100{,}000$ edge devices must identify one of $M=2^{20}$ anomaly signatures from noisy telemetry. Each device observes $n$ samples, sends a privacy-processed message capped at $12$ bits, and the service promises less than $5\%$ identification error for every signature.

Model: let $V$ index a loss-separated signature packing. The pipeline is $V\to X^n\to Z\to S\to\widehat V$, where $Z$ is the privacy channel and $S$ is the transmitted state. A Fano lower bound requires roughly $(0.95)(20)-1=18$ bits of mutual information for the claimed error, while $I(V;S)\le12$. Under this model, the contract is impossible regardless of sample count.

Risks: signatures may not be separated under the actual operational loss; side information at the server changes conditional entropy; messages across rounds may total more than $12$ bits; adaptivity changes the channel; drift invalidates the hard family; privacy accounting and mutual-information contraction are not interchangeable without proof.

Evidence: packing construction, divergence estimates from real noise, end-to-end transcript size, conditional side information, per-signature error, independence checks, privacy composition, and simulations verifying that nearest-neighbor decoding follows from the accuracy contract.

Decision: increase communication/state, weaken the signature granularity, accept larger error, or exploit justified side information. Do not promise the impossible classifier merely because a prototype succeeds on an easy empirical distribution.

Prove an Impossibility Without an Algorithm

An information-theoretic proof begins with the task’s loss and quantifiers. Construct a family of parameters separated enough that accuracy identifies a hidden label. Choose a prior, generate observations, and upper-bound the information reaching the estimator through sampling, privacy, communication, or memory. Then lower-bound the information accuracy would require and compare the two.

The final theorem must name its model: input family, distributional assumptions, number of observations, memory or message budget, randomness, side information, loss, success probability, and whether the conclusion is Bayes, average-case, or minimax.

Practice

Practice: Prove an Impossibility Without an Algorithm

Consider $M=32$ candidate parameters with pairwise loss distance greater than $2\varepsilon$. The hidden index is uniform, and an estimator succeeds within $\varepsilon$ with probability at least $3/4$.

Tier 1 — Definitions. Define entropy, conditional entropy, mutual information, KL divergence, total variation, packing, Bayes risk, and minimax risk. State one correct data-processing chain.

Tier 2 — Decoding. Prove that an $\varepsilon$-accurate estimate identifies the nearest packing point. Explain where strict separation greater than $2\varepsilon$ enters.

Tier 3 — Fano arithmetic. With $\log_2 32=5$, derive $I(V;S)\ge(3/4)(5)-1=2.75$ bits using the stated form. Conclude that a two-bit state cannot meet the promise.

Tier 4 — Statistical bound. Suppose each independent sample contributes at most $0.05$ bit of mutual information. Use data processing to obtain $n\ge55$. State why dependence could invalidate simple addition.

Tier 5 — Counterexamples. Break the argument with overlapping packing balls, server side information, a nonuniform prior of tiny entropy, easy-to-distinguish distributions, and a loss that ignores the packed coordinate. Name the failed step each time.

Tier 6 — Professional artifact. Write a proof certificate containing task and loss, hard family, prior, separation lemma, observation law, Markov chain, divergence calculation, information upper bound, Fano/testing lower bound, minimax conversion, constants, and scope limits.

Debugging prompts: if Fano is trivial, enlarge the packing or tighten information; if distributions are too distinct, reduce perturbations; if perturbations destroy loss separation, redesign the geometry; if memory seems irrelevant, check whether $I(V;X^n)$ is already the smaller bottleneck.

Stretch goal: prove the same rate with Le Cam, Fano, and Assouad where possible. Compare constants, dimension dependence, and which structure each method exposes.

Rubric (10 points): precise model 2; valid separated hard family 2; justified information/divergence upper bound 2; correct testing or Fano step 2; quantifiers, counterexamples, and scope 2.

Bridge: communication reductions view a streaming state as a message; information theory measures what that message can reveal. The next step connects these ideas to information complexity, privacy contraction, adaptive data analysis, and cell-probe lower bounds.

Impossibility checklist: define the loss; pack worlds that accuracy must separate; choose a prior; model every observation, side channel, and randomization; upper-bound information through the pipeline; lower-bound information required for testing; and convert average hardness to the claimed quantifiers. High raw-data entropy alone proves nothing about memory unless the task must preserve those distinctions.
