Why Repetition Can Turn Luck into Confidence

Imagine asking several independent juries the same yes-or-no question. If each jury reaches the correct answer with probability $0.75$, trusting one jury leaves a $25\%$ failure risk. Trusting the majority of many juries is much safer: the final answer is wrong only when unusually many juries are wrong together. A concentration bound quantifies how unusual that collective failure is.

This deep dive builds on basic probability and concentration. The prerequisite chapter establishes random variables, expectation, independence, Markov's inequality, and variance-based bounds. The gap here is sharper control: variance alone often gives a failure probability that decreases only polynomially, whereas Chernoff's method turns independence and boundedness into an exponentially decreasing tail. That exponential tail is what makes confidence amplification practical.

For a tiny example, repeat an algorithm three independent times when each run succeeds with probability $3/4$, then take a majority vote. The vote fails if exactly two or all three runs fail:

$$ \Pr[\text{majority fails}] =\binom{3}{2}\left(\frac14\right)^2\left(\frac34\right) +\left(\frac14\right)^3 =\frac{10}{64}=0.15625. $$

The improvement is modest with three runs, but the probability falls exponentially as the odd number of independent repetitions grows. The essential words are important. A tail event is a large deviation from the mean. A moment-generating function (MGF) is $M_X(t)=\mathbb E[e^{tX}]$ where finite. Amplification converts a constant-success procedure into a high-confidence procedure. A union bound protects many decisions at once by adding their individual failure probabilities. A median is robust when a majority of numerical estimates are good; a majority vote is its Boolean analogue.

The motivating questions are therefore operational as well as mathematical. How many repetitions achieve failure probability $\delta$? Which form of the tail bound fits the deviation being controlled? Can randomness truly be treated as independent? How should a global error budget be divided across millions of answers? What changes when estimates are unbounded or heavy-tailed?

Independent repetitions do not improve the quality of an individual run; they make it exponentially unlikely that a majority of runs fail together. Repeating the same randomness, correlated failures, or a shared systematic bias does not provide this guarantee.

Warm-up

Why Repetition Can Turn Luck into Confidence

An estimator is good with probability $3/4$. Run it independently $r=5$ times and take the median.

  1. Let $B$ be the number of bad runs. The aggregate is bad only if $B\ge3$.
  2. Compute the exact probability: $$ \Pr[B\ge3]=\binom53(1/4)^3(3/4)^2+\binom54(1/4)^4(3/4)+(1/4)^5=\frac{106}{1024}\approx0.1035. $$
  3. Checkpoint: one or two bad values cannot move the median outside the good interval; three can.
  4. Compare one run: failure $0.25$. Five-run amplification reduces it to about $0.1035$.
  5. Boundary test: if all runs reuse one random seed, all five outcomes can be identical and failure remains $0.25$.

Reflection: what operational dependency—shared data corruption, shared seed, or common model bias—could make the binomial calculation invalid?

The Moment-Generating-Function Route

Let $X=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$, where independent $X_i\in\{0,1\}$ have means $p_i$, and let $\mu=\mathbb E[X]=\sum_i p_i$. We derive the upper tail in auditable steps.

For any $t\gt0$, exponentiation preserves the event $X\ge a$. Markov's inequality applied to the nonnegative random variable $e^{tX}$ gives

$$ \Pr[X\ge a]=\Pr[e^{tX}\ge e^{ta}]\le e^{-ta}\mathbb E[e^{tX}]. $$

Independence is used at exactly one decisive point:

$$ \mathbb E[e^{tX}]=\mathbb E\!\left[\prod_i e^{tX_i}\right]=\prod_i\mathbb E[e^{tX_i}]. $$

For Bernoulli $X_i$, $\mathbb E[e^{tX_i}]=1-p_i+p_i e^t=1+p_i(e^t-1)$. Using $1+u\le e^u$ yields

$$ \mathbb E[e^{tX}]\le\exp\!\left((e^t-1)\sum_i p_i\right)=\exp(\mu(e^t-1)). $$

Set $a=(1+\varepsilon)\mu$. We may choose any $t\gt0$, so minimize the exponent $-t(1+\varepsilon)\mu+\mu(e^t-1)$. Its derivative vanishes at $e^t=1+\varepsilon$, hence $t=\ln(1+\varepsilon)$. Substitution gives the exact multiplicative form

$$ \Pr[X\ge(1+\varepsilon)\mu] \le\left(\frac{e^\varepsilon}{(1+\varepsilon)^{1+\varepsilon}}\right)^\mu. $$

For $0\le\varepsilon\le1$, a standard relaxation gives $\Pr[X\ge(1+\varepsilon)\mu]\le e^{-\mu\varepsilon^2/3}$. A lower-tail derivation uses $t\lt0$ and gives $\Pr[X\le(1-\varepsilon)\mu]\le e^{-\mu\varepsilon^2/2}$ for $0\le\varepsilon\le1$. Combining tails by a union bound yields the memorable two-sided estimate $2e^{-\mu\varepsilon^2/3}$.

Every assumption has a job. Nonnegativity enables Markov on $e^{tX}$; the MGF must be finite at the chosen $t$; independence factorizes the MGF; bounded Bernoulli structure supplies the factor bound; and the simplified constant $1/3$ uses the range $0\le\varepsilon\le1$. If $X_i=Z$ for every $i$ with one Bernoulli $Z$, then $X=nZ$ and a majority does not concentrate around $np$: independence has failed. Hoeffding is a nearby alternative that handles independent bounded variables with unequal ranges and emphasizes additive deviation, often avoiding dependence on $\mu$.

The convenient $e^{-\mu\varepsilon^2/3}$ upper tail is valid for $0\le\varepsilon\le1$ and can be loose; it is not a substitute for checking independence, the deviation regime, or the sharper exact form.

Multiplicative Tails and Median Amplification

Consider $X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(1000,0.2)$, so $\mu=200$. We want $\Pr[X\ge260]$, corresponding to $\varepsilon=0.3$. The exact Chernoff expression is

$$ \left(\frac{e^{0.3}}{1.3^{1.3}}\right)^{200}\approx0.000271, $$

while the simplified upper bound is $e^{-200(0.3)^2/3}=e^{-6}\approx0.002479$. Both are rigorous upper bounds; neither is the exact binomial probability. Directly summing binomial masses gives approximately $2.58\times10^{-6}$, illustrating the price of a general-purpose inequality.

Now move from a sum to amplification. Suppose one numerical estimator lands in $[\theta-\alpha,\theta+\alpha]$ with probability at least $3/4$. Repeat it independently $r$ times and return the median. Define $B_i=1$ when run $i$ is bad. Then $\mathbb E[\sum_iB_i]\le r/4$, and a bad median requires at least $r/2$ bad runs—twice the worst-case mean. Applying the exact Chernoff form with $\varepsilon=1$ gives

$$ \Pr[\text{bad median}]\le\left(\frac e4\right)^{r/4}=e^{-cr}, \qquad c=\frac{\ln(4/e)}4\approx0.0966. $$

Thus $r\ge\ln(1/\delta)/c$ is sufficient. Constants can be sharpened by a direct binomial or relative-entropy bound, but the structural conclusion is $r=O(\log(1/\delta))$. Use an odd $r$ to avoid median tie conventions. Independence must include random seeds and any sampled data assumed fresh; deterministic systematic error remains after amplification.

Worked Example

Multiplicative Tails and Median Amplification

Tail calculation. Let $X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(1000,0.2)$ and test $X\ge260$.

  1. $\mu=np=200$ and $260=(1+0.3)\mu$.
  2. Simplified Chernoff: $e^{-\mu\varepsilon^2/3}=e^{-6}\approx0.002479$.
  3. Exact-form Chernoff: $[e^{0.3}/1.3^{1.3}]^{200}\approx0.000271$.
  4. Independent check: sum $\sum_{k=260}^{1000}\binom{1000}{k}0.2^k0.8^{1000-k}\approx2.58\times10^{-6}$.
  5. Invariant: the exact probability must not exceed either valid bound.

Amplification. A run fails with probability $q=0.25$. For $r=11$, majority failure is $\sum_{k=6}^{11}\binom{11}{k}q^k(1-q)^{11-k}\approx0.03433$.

Perturbed case: with $q=0.4$, the same 11-run failure becomes approximately $0.2465$. The method still improves confidence because $q\lt1/2$, but much more slowly. At $q=1/2$, majority repetition cannot amplify.

from math import comb, exp, log
from random import Random

def binomial_upper_tail(n, p, threshold):
    if not (isinstance(n, int) and n >= 0):
        raise ValueError("n must be a nonnegative integer")
    if not (0.0 <= p <= 1.0):
        raise ValueError("p must lie in [0, 1]")
    if not (isinstance(threshold, int) and 0 <= threshold <= n + 1):
        raise ValueError("threshold must lie in [0, n + 1]")
    return sum(comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p)**(n - k)
               for k in range(threshold, n + 1))

def chernoff_upper(n, p, threshold):
    mu = n * p
    if mu == 0:
        return 0.0 if threshold > 0 else 1.0
    epsilon = threshold / mu - 1
    if epsilon < 0:
        raise ValueError("threshold must be at least the mean")
    exact_form = (exp(epsilon) /
                  (1 + epsilon)**(1 + epsilon))**mu
    simple = exp(-mu * epsilon**2 / 3) if epsilon <= 1 else None
    return exact_form, simple

def majority_failure(r, q):
    if r <= 0 or r % 2 == 0:
        raise ValueError("r must be a positive odd integer")
    return binomial_upper_tail(r, q, r // 2 + 1)

#Normal case: independent exact calculation and two valid bounds.
actual = binomial_upper_tail(1000, 0.2, 260)
sharp, simple = chernoff_upper(1000, 0.2, 260)
print("binomial tail:", actual)
print("Chernoff exact-form / simple:", sharp, simple)
assert actual <= sharp <= simple

#Amplification and boundary cases.
print("11-run majority failure, q=.25:", majority_failure(11, 0.25))
print("11-run majority failure, q=.40:", majority_failure(11, 0.40))
assert majority_failure(11, 0.0) == 0.0
assert majority_failure(11, 0.5) == 0.5

#Deterministic simulation with genuinely fresh per-run draws.
rng = Random(20260927)
trials, r, q = 100_000, 11, 0.25
observed = sum(sum(rng.random() < q for _ in range(r)) > r // 2
               for _ in range(trials)) / trials
print("simulated majority failure:", observed)
assert abs(observed - majority_failure(r, q)) < 0.003

#Invalid and adversarial tests: reject bad input; expose shared randomness.
try:
    majority_failure(10, 0.25)
except ValueError as err:
    print("invalid test passed:", err)
shared_failure = q  # all 11 runs copy one Bernoulli outcome
print("shared-randomness failure (no amplification):", shared_failure)

Sizing Confidence Across Many Decisions

In a teaching case, suppose a randomized primality subroutine has one-sided error at most $1/4$ per independent round. Repeating $r$ rounds and rejecting when any round finds a witness can drive the false-prime probability to $(1/4)^r$. This is stronger than median amplification because one-sided error permits an AND rule.

At professional scale, imagine a streaming analytics service publishes $m=10^6$ daily estimates and wants the probability of any inaccurate publication that day to be at most $10^{-3}$. A union bound allocates $\delta_j=10^{-9}$ per estimate. If a base sketch succeeds with probability $3/4$, median amplification needs $r=O(\log(10^9))$ independent copies; using the conservative constant above gives $r\ge215$, rounded upward to an odd number. A sharper binomial calculation may materially reduce that engineering cost. The allocation need not be uniform: safety-critical outputs may receive smaller $\delta_j$, provided the sum stays within the global budget.

The failure case is shared dependence. If all replicas hash with the same seed, experience the same poisoned partition, or inherit the same biased training data, their errors can align. Conditional independence may also be insufficient if the conditioning variable represents a widespread outage. Evidence that invalidates selection includes correlated residuals, empirical coverage below target, drift outside the workload model, seed collisions, or observed tail frequency above the confidence envelope.

For production use, distinguish theorem probability from service reliability. Chernoff bounds describe modeled randomness; they do not include software bugs, silent data loss, adversarial seed discovery, numeric overflow, or an incorrect oracle. Those risks need fault isolation, tests, monitoring, and incident budgets alongside probabilistic sizing.

Case Study

Sizing Confidence Across Many Decisions: Chernoff Bounds & Confidence Amplification

Scenario. A telemetry platform answers $m=100{,}000$ high-value queries per hour. The hourly probability that any published answer violates its accuracy interval must be at most $10^{-4}$. One sketch instance is accurate with probability at least $0.75$ under the validated workload. A replica costs $12$ MB and $0.15$ ms per update when processed serially.

  1. Allocate a uniform budget $\delta_q=10^{-4}/10^5=10^{-9}$ per query. No independence across queries is needed for this union bound.
  2. Use $c=\ln(4/e)/4\approx0.0966$ and solve $e^{-cr}\le10^{-9}$: $r\ge214.5$, hence conservative odd choice $r=215$.
  3. Resource checkpoint: $215\times12$ MB $=2580$ MB. Serial update work is $32.25$ ms, likely unacceptable. This proves feasibility mathematically but may fail the system SLO.
  4. Recalculate with the exact binomial tail $\Pr[\operatorname{Bin}(r,0.25)\ge(r+1)/2]$ and choose the smallest odd $r$ below $10^{-9}$; the code widget can perform the search. Benchmark that candidate rather than assuming the loose constant is affordable.
  5. Validation protocol: use independent seeds, replay representative and adversarial streams, retain exact answers on a sample, measure empirical bad-run correlation, and report one-sided confidence intervals for observed failures. Inject duplicate seeds and a common corrupted partition to confirm monitors detect dependence.
  6. Expected observations: aggregate failures fall exponentially only in the isolated-seed condition; duplicate seeds create clusters of simultaneous failures; memory and latency grow linearly with $r$.

Selection decision: deploy only the smallest exactly sized $r$ that meets accuracy, memory, and p99 update SLOs, with seed-isolation and coverage alarms. Reject median amplification alone if shared-mode failures dominate; redesign isolation or use a deterministic fallback.

Derive and Stress-Test a Tail Guarantee

Mastery means being able to reconstruct the proof, choose the correct inequality, audit its assumptions, calculate constants, and decide whether the guarantee survives contact with a real system. Work progressively: first derive on paper, then verify exact values, then attack the model.

The conceptual bridge backward is Markov's inequality: Chernoff is Markov applied after an exponential transformation. The bridge forward is to randomized algorithms, streaming sketches, learning-theory generalization bounds, differential privacy accounting, and robust estimation. In each setting, the same design pattern appears: define a bad event, create a tractable potential or MGF, bound one event, then compose confidence across repetitions or decisions.

Practice

Practice: Derive and Stress-Test a Tail Guarantee

Starter inputs: independent $X_i\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.1)$ for $i=1,\ldots,500$, target $X\ge75$, base-estimator failure $q=0.2$, and system target $\delta=10^{-6}$ across $m=1000$ outputs.

Tier 1 — Recall and calculate

  1. Compute $\mu=50$ and $\varepsilon=75/50-1=0.5$.
  2. Evaluate the simplified upper tail $e^{-50(0.5)^2/3}=e^{-25/6}\approx0.01550$.
  3. State where independence and $0\le\varepsilon\le1$ are used.

Expected artifact: one annotated calculation with event, mean, deviation regime, theorem statement, and bound. Completion criterion: every symbol has a value or definition.

Tier 2 — Rebuild the proof

  1. Start from $\Pr[e^{tX}\ge e^{75t}]$.
  2. Apply Markov, factor the MGF, bound $1+p(e^t-1)$ by $e^{p(e^t-1)}$, and optimize $t$.
  3. Compare the exact-form Chernoff value with the simplified value and the exact binomial tail.

Hint: solve $e^t=1+\varepsilon$. Debugging prompt: if the optimized $t$ is negative for an upper tail, check the threshold and derivative.

Tier 3 — Amplify and compose

  1. Allocate $\delta_j=10^{-9}$ so the union bound across $1000$ outputs is at most $10^{-6}$.
  2. Find the smallest odd $r$ for which $\Pr[\operatorname{Bin}(r,0.2)\ge(r+1)/2]\le10^{-9}$.
  3. Compare this exact sizing with a conservative Chernoff sizing, recording both resource costs.

Expected artifact: a small table of $r$, exact failure probability, memory, and latency. Completion criterion: the selected $r$ meets the per-output budget and the summed budget.

Tier 4 — Break the assumptions

Test four cases: fresh seeds; all replicas share one seed; replicas form correlated pairs; and base failure rises to $0.49$. Explain why the binomial model applies or fails. Add invalid inputs such as even $r$, $q\gt1$, and a threshold below the mean to the verifier.

Edge cases: $q=0$, $q=1/2$, $\mu=0$, tiny $\mu$, and $\varepsilon\gt1$. Completion criterion: invalid theorem use is rejected rather than silently producing a reassuring number.

Tier 5 — Professional judgment and extension

Design a nonuniform risk allocation for outputs of three severities. Then replace bounded observations with finite-variance heavy-tailed data and outline a median-of-means estimator: number of blocks, block size, independence requirements, and evidence needed to choose it. State one observation that would invalidate each design.

Rubric (10 points): assumptions 2; derivation 2; numerical verification 2; adversarial tests 2; operational decision and invalidation evidence 2. A complete submission earns at least 8 points and contains no unsupported independence claim.

Reflection: is the dominant residual risk random fluctuation, model mismatch, or shared operational failure—and does repetition address it?

Decision checklist: define the bad event, choose additive or multiplicative scale, verify boundedness/MGF and independence, retain constants, size $r$ and union-bound budgets, then test correlation and system cost. Do not confuse a bound with the exact probability or confidence amplification with bias removal; the next step is applying this pattern to sketch-specific guarantees and robust estimators such as median-of-means.
