The Meaning of Life

September 10, 2026 Wasil Zafar 6 min read

Religious, secular, and existentialist perspectives on life's meaning

Table of Contents

  1. Religious Perspectives: Meaning as Given
  2. Secular Perspectives: Meaning Without a Metaphysical Anchor
  3. Existentialist Perspectives: Meaning as a Project
  4. A Side-by-Side Comparison
  5. Closing Thought

Few questions have occupied humanity longer, or been answered more differently, than "what is the meaning of life?" It isn't really one question — it's a bundle of them: is meaning given to us, or made by us? Does it require a purpose beyond ourselves, or can it live entirely within a single human life? Three broad traditions — religious, secular, and existentialist — have staked out genuinely different answers, and the disagreement between them is more interesting than any one of them taken alone.

Religious Perspectives: Meaning as Given

Across most religious traditions, meaning isn't something a person invents — it's something they discover, because it was placed there by something larger than themselves.

Core Insight: the religious answer to "why does my life matter?" is almost always structural, not personal — your life matters because of where it sits inside something bigger (a creator's plan, a moral order, a cycle of rebirth), not purely because of what you, individually, decide to do with it.

In the Abrahamic traditions (Judaism, Christianity, Islam), meaning typically flows from relationship and covenant: a life lived in right relationship with God, in service to others, and in obedience to a moral order that predates and outlasts the individual. Suffering and mortality are real, but they're framed inside a larger story — one with a beginning, an author, and (for most of these traditions) a continuation beyond death.

Dharmic traditions (Hinduism, Buddhism) tell a different kind of larger story. Meaning is bound up with dharma (one's duty or right way of being) and, ultimately, liberation — moksha or nirvana — from a cycle of rebirth driven by desire and attachment. Here the goal isn't so much "fulfilling a plan" as "seeing clearly": suffering persists because we misunderstand the nature of the self, and meaning comes from the long work of correcting that misunderstanding.

What both share, despite their real differences, is the conviction that meaning is discovered rather than invented — that a life can be lived well or badly measured against a standard that doesn't originate with the person living it.

Secular Perspectives: Meaning Without a Metaphysical Anchor

Secular accounts of meaning drop the metaphysical anchor — no creator, no cosmic plan, no rebirth — without concluding that life is therefore meaningless. Instead, they relocate meaning inside human life itself: relationships, achievement, the reduction of suffering, the flourishing of the species.

Secular humanism argues that meaning comes from human flourishing and human agency: we are the authors of value because we are the only value-caring beings we know of, and that's enough. A version closer to classical utilitarianism narrows this further — a meaningful life is one that increases well-being and reduces suffering, for oneself and for others, measured in the plainest terms available: happiness, health, connection, the absence of needless pain.

A more scientifically-minded secular view goes further still, treating meaning as an emergent, evolved feature of a social species — humans built to find purpose in cooperation, reputation, and care for offspring, because those tendencies were adaptive. On this account, "meaning" is real as a psychological and social fact, even though it isn't written into the structure of the universe.

Reflection

A Question Worth Sitting With

If meaning is something humans construct rather than discover, does that make it less real — or does it just mean we're responsible for it in a way religious accounts don't require?

Existentialist Perspectives: Meaning as a Project

Existentialism sits in a strange middle position: it agrees with the secular view that there's no cosmic plan waiting to be discovered, but it refuses the comfortable move of saying human flourishing simply fills the gap. For thinkers like Jean-Paul Sartre, "existence precedes essence" — we're thrown into existence first, with no predetermined nature or purpose, and only afterward do we define ourselves, through the choices we actually make. Meaning, on this view, isn't found or assumed; it's a project, something each person is personally responsible for building, with no excuse and no one else to blame if they fail to.

Albert Camus pushed the problem further with the idea of the absurd: the collision between our deep hunger for meaning and a universe that offers none. His famous answer wasn't despair, but defiance — Sisyphus, condemned to push his boulder up the hill forever, becomes (in Camus's telling) something close to happy, because he embraces the task fully rather than waiting for a justification that will never come.

What makes existentialism distinct from secular humanism isn't the absence of God — both agree on that — but its refusal to treat "human well-being" as a ready-made substitute. Existentialists insist the absence of given meaning is something to be confronted honestly, not smoothed over.

Closing Thought

None of these three traditions has "won" the argument, and it's worth asking whether the question is even the kind that admits of a single correct answer, the way a scientific question does. What they share, despite everything that separates them, is a refusal to treat the question as unimportant. Whether meaning is discovered, cultivated, or built by hand, every one of these traditions agrees on one thing: a life examined is different from a life merely lived.
